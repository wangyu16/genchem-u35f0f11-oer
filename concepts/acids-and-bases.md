# Chapter 15 Concept Map: Acids and Bases

## Source and Scope

**Reference:** instructor's own lecture notes (Yu Wang, University of Louisiana at Lafayette),
Chapter 15 (CC BY 4.0). **Audience:** introductory undergraduate general chemistry. **Note:** covers
General Concepts (Brønsted acids/bases, water's acid-base properties, acid/base strength),
Structure and Property Correlation, Lewis Acids and Bases, and pH Related Calculations. Simple
Markdown only — no images, diagrams, or orz containers.

## Chapter Summary

Chapter 15 applies Chapter 14's general equilibrium toolkit to the single most common equilibrium
type in the course: acid-base chemistry. It opens by formalizing the Brønsted acid/base definitions
(first introduced in Chapter 4) as conjugate acid-base pairs, and treats water's self-ionization
(Kw) as the reference equilibrium underlying the pH scale. Strong acids/bases (complete ionization)
are distinguished from weak acids/bases (partial ionization, governed by Ka or Kb), with the
chapter's structure-property section explaining *why* acid strength varies — bond strength in
hydrohalic acids, and central-atom electronegativity/oxidation state in oxoacids — extending
Chapter 8's periodic-trend reasoning to a new context. Lewis acid-base theory then generalizes the
definition beyond proton transfer to electron-pair donation/acceptance.

The chapter's quantitative core is pH-related calculations: finding pH for strong acids/bases
(direct), weak acids/bases (ICE table with Ka/Kb, identical in method to Chapter 14), diprotic/
polyprotic acids (stepwise ionization), and salt solutions (hydrolysis, using KaKb = Kw). This
chapter's ICE-table method is the direct and repeated template for Chapter 16's buffers,
titrations, and solubility equilibria — nothing new is introduced there except which species are
present.

## Core Dependency Chain

- **Conjugate acid-base pairs and Kw**
  - This enables: pH/pOH calculations for any aqueous system, and the KaKb = Kw relationship used
    throughout salt-hydrolysis and buffer problems.
- **Strong vs. weak acid/base classification (memorized list + Ka/Kb)**
  - This enables: Ch. 16 — buffers require a weak acid/base pair; titration curve shape depends on
    whether the acid/base being titrated is strong or weak.
- **Acid-strength structure-property trends**
  - Reinforces Ch. 8's periodic-trend reasoning in a new context; not a heavy prerequisite for
    later chapters, but a recurring conceptual thread.
- **pH calculations via ICE tables (weak acids/bases, polyprotic acids, salt hydrolysis)**
  - This enables: Ch. 16 — buffer pH, titration-curve pH, and solubility calculations are all this
    same ICE-table method applied to a new set of species.

### 1. General Concepts (§15.1)

**Learning objectives:**
- **Objective 15.1a** — Identify conjugate acid-base pairs and diprotic acid ionization steps
- **Objective 15.1b** — Calculate pH/pOH and recognize strong/weak acids and bases

**Concept flow:**
- Brønsted acid (proton donor) and base (proton acceptor) → conjugate acid-base pairs (differ by
  one H⁺)
- Water autoionization (Kw = [H⁺][OH⁻] = 1.0×10⁻¹⁴ at 25 °C) → defines the pH scale (pH = −log[H⁺];
  pH + pOH = 14)
- Strong acids/bases (memorized list, ionize completely) vs. weak acids/bases (ionize partially,
  governed by Ka/Kb)

### 2. Structure and Property Correlation (§15.2)

**Learning objectives:**
- **Objective 15.2a** — Explain hydrohalic acid and oxoacid strength trends
- **Objective 15.2b** — Predict salt solution acidity and classify oxides

**Concept flow:**
- Hydrohalic acids → weaker bond gives stronger acid (HI strongest, HF weakest)
- Oxoacids → acid strength increases with central-atom electronegativity (same group) or with
  oxidation number/number of oxygens (same central atom)
- Salts → strong acid + strong base → neutral; strong base + weak acid → basic; strong acid +
  weak base → acidic; weak acid + weak base → compare Ka and Kb
- Oxides → acidic, basic, or amphoteric (extends Ch. 8's oxide classification)

### 3. Lewis Acids and Bases (§15.3)

**Learning objectives:**
- **Objective 15.3a** — Identify Lewis acids and bases

**Concept flow:**
- Lewis acid-base theory → generalizes Brønsted theory beyond proton transfer to any electron-
  pair donation/acceptance
- Recognizing Lewis acids/bases in reactions where no proton is transferred (e.g., metal cations
  accepting a lone pair)

### 4. pH Related Calculations (§15.4)

**Learning objectives:**
- **Objective 15.4a** — Calculate pH for strong acid/base solutions
- **Objective 15.4b** — Calculate pH for weak acid/base solutions and percent ionization
- **Objective 15.4c** — Calculate equilibrium concentrations for a diprotic/polyprotic acid
- **Objective 15.4d** — Calculate the pH of a salt solution using hydrolysis

**Concept flow:**
- Strong acid/base → [H⁺] or [OH⁻] equals initial concentration directly (complete ionization)
- Weak acid/base → ICE table with Ka or Kb (identical method to Ch. 14) → equilibrium [H⁺] or
  [OH⁻] → pH
  - Percent ionization = (ionized concentration at equilibrium / initial concentration) × 100%
- Diprotic/polyprotic acids → stepwise ICE tables, first step usually dominates [H⁺]
- Salt hydrolysis → identify which ion hydrolyzes, use KaKb = Kw to find the relevant equilibrium
  constant, then apply the same ICE-table method

## Common Student Bottlenecks

| Bottleneck | Conceptual repair |
|---|---|
| Forgetting the memorized strong-acid/strong-base list and instead trying to calculate pH via an ICE table for a strong acid | Always ask "is this on the strong acid/base list?" as the first step of any pH problem, before deciding whether an ICE table is even needed. |
| Confusing acid strength (Ka, a chemical property) with acid concentration (molarity, an amount) | Keep the two ideas explicitly separate with paired examples (e.g., a concentrated weak acid vs. a dilute strong acid) so students don't conflate "more acid" with "stronger acid." |
| Applying the oxoacid electronegativity trend and the oxoacid oxidation-number trend as if they were the same rule | Separate the two comparison types explicitly: same central atom, different oxygen count (oxidation-number trend) vs. same oxidation number, different central atom (electronegativity trend). |
| Skipping the second ionization step of a diprotic acid, or over-weighting its contribution to [H⁺] | Teach the general pattern explicitly: because Ka2 ≪ Ka1 for most diprotic acids, [H⁺] is set almost entirely by the first step, and the second step is solved afterward using [H⁺] from the first. |
| Misidentifying which ion in a salt actually hydrolyzes (e.g., assuming the metal cation always hydrolyzes) | Have students check each ion against the strong-acid/strong-base list separately: only the conjugate of a *weak* acid or base hydrolyzes significantly. |
