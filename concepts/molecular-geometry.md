## Chapter Summary

Chapter 10 takes the Lewis structures built in Chapter 9 and asks what three-dimensional shape they
imply. The valence-shell electron-pair repulsion (VSEPR) model answers this by treating both
bonding and lone electron pairs as mutually repelling regions around a central atom, giving a
systematic set of shapes (linear, trigonal planar, tetrahedral, trigonal bipyramidal, octahedral,
and their lone-pair-modified variants such as bent, trigonal pyramidal, and see-saw). Once geometry
is known, dipole moments can be assessed: a molecule is polar only if it has polar bonds *and*
those bond dipoles do not cancel by symmetry — geometry, not bond polarity alone, decides overall
molecular polarity.

The second half of the chapter explains *why* these geometries arise mechanistically, via valence
bond theory and orbital hybridization (sp, sp², sp³), including how double and triple bonds are
built from one sigma bond plus one or two pi bonds. This chapter's geometry and polarity results
feed directly into Chapter 11: whether a molecule is polar (from this chapter) determines which
intermolecular force(s) it can participate in (dipole-dipole, hydrogen bonding, or dispersion-only)
in the next chapter.

### 1. Molecular Geometry: The VSEPR Model (§10.1)

**Learning objectives:**
- **Objective 10.1a** — Predict molecular geometry for a central atom with no lone pairs
- **Objective 10.1b** — Predict molecular geometry for a central atom with lone pairs

**Concept flow:**
- VSEPR principle (electron pairs repel; double/triple bonds counted as one repelling domain) →
  electron-domain geometry
- No lone pairs on central atom → AB₂ (linear), AB₃ (trigonal planar), AB₄ (tetrahedral), AB₅
  (trigonal bipyramidal), AB₆ (octahedral)
- One or more lone pairs → same electron-domain geometry, but molecular shape name changes (e.g.,
  bent, trigonal pyramidal, see-saw, T-shaped, square pyramidal)
- Multiple central atoms → geometry described locally, around each central atom separately

### 2. Dipole Moments (§10.2)

**Learning objectives:**
- **Objective 10.2a** — Determine polarity of diatomic molecules
- **Objective 10.2b** — Determine polarity of polyatomic molecules

**Concept flow:**
- Dipole moment (μ = Q × r) → diatomic molecules of different elements are polar; same-element
  diatomics are nonpolar
- Polyatomic molecule polarity requires two conditions: polar bonds present, AND those bond
  dipoles do not cancel by symmetry (geometry from Section 1 determines cancellation)

### 3. Valence Bond Theory and Hybridization (§10.3)

**Learning objectives:**
- **Objective 10.3a** — Determine hybridization using valence bond theory
- **Objective 10.3b** — Identify sigma and pi bonds and apply the multiple-bond hybridization rule

**Concept flow:**
- Valence bond theory → covalent bond forms when atomic orbitals overlap and the system reaches a
  potential-energy minimum
- Hybridization (mixing of atomic orbitals) → sp (linear, 2 domains), sp² (trigonal planar, 3
  domains), sp³ (tetrahedral, 4 domains)
- Double bonds → one sigma bond (hybrid orbital overlap) + one pi bond (unhybridized p-orbital
  sideways overlap); triple bonds → one sigma + two pi bonds
  - Rule: a double bond → sp²-hybridized central atom; two double bonds or a triple bond →
    sp-hybridized central atom (second-period elements)

## Common Student Bottlenecks

| Bottleneck | Conceptual repair |
|---|---|
| Confusing electron-domain geometry with molecular (shape) geometry when lone pairs are present | Teach the two-step process explicitly: first identify the electron-domain geometry (all domains, including lone pairs), then name the molecular shape based on only the atom positions. |
| Assuming a molecule with polar bonds is automatically polar | Require both conditions to be checked every time: are the bonds polar, and does the molecular geometry allow the dipoles to cancel (e.g., linear/symmetric arrangements often cancel). |
| Treating double/triple bonds as "bigger" single-bond domains that count more toward repulsion | State explicitly that VSEPR counts a multiple bond as *one* repelling domain, same as a single bond, when predicting geometry. |
| Assigning hybridization by memorizing examples rather than counting electron domains | Always count the number of electron domains (bonding + lone pairs) around the central atom first; hybridization follows directly from that count (2→sp, 3→sp², 4→sp³). |
| Confusing sigma and pi bonds, or miscounting them in a double/triple bond | Anchor with a fixed rule: every single bond has exactly one sigma bond; every additional bond in a double/triple bond is a pi bond, always formed from unhybridized p orbitals. |
