# Chapter 18 Concept Map: Electrochemistry

**Reference:** instructor's own lecture notes (Yu Wang, University of Louisiana at Lafayette),
Chapter 18 (CC BY 4.0). **Audience:** introductory undergraduate general chemistry. **Note:** covers
Redox Reactions (oxidation numbers, balancing redox equations), Galvanic Cells, Standard Reduction

Markdown only — no images, diagrams, or orz containers.

## Chapter Summary

Chapter 18 is the course's synthesis chapter: it takes redox reactions (first introduced in Chapter
4), equilibrium constants (Chapter 14), and free energy (Chapter 17) and unites them into a single
quantitative framework built around electron transfer. It opens by reviewing and extending redox
balancing — the half-reaction method for acidic and basic solutions — before introducing the
galvanic cell as a device that physically separates oxidation (anode) and reduction (cathode) so
the electron transfer does useful electrical work.

Standard reduction potentials (E°) let any two half-reactions be combined to predict overall cell
reaction, cell diagram, and cell potential, with the higher-E° half-reaction always acting as the
cathode. The chapter's central equations then connect three quantities that describe the same
reaction from three different theoretical angles: ΔG° = −nFE° (linking cell potential to free
energy, Chapter 17) and E° = (RT/nF) ln K (linking cell potential to the equilibrium constant,
Chapter 14). The Nernst equation extends E° to non-standard concentrations, including concentration
cells. The chapter closes with three practical applications — corrosion, electrolysis, and
batteries — that put the whole framework to work.

- **Redox balancing (half-reaction method, acidic and basic solutions)**
  - This enables: writing correct overall reactions and cell diagrams for any galvanic cell problem
    in this chapter.

  - This enables: calculating standard cell potential (E°cell = E°cathode − E°anode) for any pair
    of half-reactions.

  - This enables: converting freely between ΔG° (Ch. 17), K (Ch. 14), and E° for the same

- **Nernst equation**
  - This enables: calculating cell potential under non-standard conditions and for concentration
    cells, extending the standard-state results to realistic scenarios.
- **Applications (corrosion, electrolysis, batteries)**
  - Terminal, illustrative applications of the framework above; not a prerequisite for later
    material.

### 1. Redox Reactions (§18.1)

**Learning objectives:**
- **Objective 18.1a** — Assign oxidation numbers
- **Objective 18.1b** — Balance redox equations using the half-reaction method

**Concept flow:**
- Oxidation (electron loss, oxidation number increases) + reduction (electron gain, oxidation
  number decreases) → always paired
- Half-reaction method → separate into oxidation/reduction half-reactions, balance atoms (other
  than O/H), balance O with H₂O and H with H⁺, balance charge with electrons, equalize electrons
  between half-reactions, combine
- Basic solution adaptation → add OH⁻ to neutralize H⁺ on both sides, combine to form water

### 2. Galvanic Cells (§18.2)

**Learning objectives:**
- **Objective 18.2a** — Define galvanic cells and write cell diagrams

**Concept flow:**
- Galvanic cell (spontaneous redox reaction generates electricity) → anode (oxidation) and cathode
  (reduction), connected by an external circuit and a salt bridge
- Cell diagram notation (anode | anode solution || cathode solution | cathode) → standardized way
  to represent a galvanic cell

### 3. Standard Reduction Potentials (§18.3)

**Learning objectives:**
- **Objective 18.3a** — Define standard reduction potential and the SHE
- **Objective 18.3b** — Identify cathode/anode and calculate standard cell potential

**Concept flow:**
- Standard reduction potential (E°, referenced to SHE = 0 V) → tabulated for half-reactions
  written as reductions
- Higher E° → cathode (reduction as written); lower E° → anode (reaction reversed, oxidation)
- E°cell = E°cathode − E°anode → always positive for a spontaneous galvanic cell

### 4. Nernst Equation (§18.4)

**Learning objectives:**
- **Objective 18.4a** — Relate ΔG°, K, and E° for a redox reaction
- **Objective 18.4b** — Apply the Nernst equation
- **Objective 18.4c** — Calculate the potential of a concentration cell

**Concept flow:**
- ΔG° = −nFE° (links E° to Ch. 17's free energy) and E° = (RT/nF) ln K (links E° to Ch. 14's
  equilibrium constant) → three equivalent descriptions of the same reaction's favorability
- Nernst equation (non-standard conditions) → E = E° − (RT/nF) ln Q
- Concentration cell (same electrode/ion, different concentrations) → E° = 0, so E = −(RT/nF) ln Q
  entirely

### 5. Applications (§18.5)

**Learning objectives:**
- **Objective 18.5a** — Explain corrosion, electrolysis, and batteries

**Concept flow:**
- Corrosion → an unwanted, spontaneous electrochemical (redox) process
- Electrolysis → electrical energy drives a nonspontaneous redox reaction (the reverse of a
  galvanic cell's spontaneous direction)
- Batteries → practical galvanic cells (or cell series) engineered for a specific, sustained
  application

## Common Student Bottlenecks

| Bottleneck | Conceptual repair |
|---|---|
| Reversing which half-reaction is the anode vs. cathode | Anchor to one fixed, non-negotiable rule: the half-reaction with the *higher* standard reduction potential is always the cathode, computed by comparison every time — never by intuition about "which metal is more reactive." |
| Forgetting to reverse the sign of E° (and the reaction direction) for the anode half-reaction before combining | Require students to explicitly rewrite the anode half-reaction as an oxidation (reversed from the tabulated reduction) as a separate step before adding the two half-reactions together. |
| Using n (moles of electrons transferred) incorrectly — forgetting to equalize electrons between half-reactions before combining | Have students explicitly multiply each half-reaction by the smallest integers needed to equalize electron count, and confirm this final n is what is used in ΔG°, K, and Nernst-equation calculations. |
| Confusing when to use E° (standard conditions) versus the Nernst equation (any other conditions) | Ask explicitly: are all solutes at 1 M and gases at 1 atm? If not, the Nernst equation is required, not E° alone. |
| Believing that a nonspontaneous reaction (negative E°cell) simply "does not happen" rather than requiring external energy input | Introduce electrolysis explicitly as the resolution: a nonspontaneous redox reaction can still occur if it is driven by an external power source, which is precisely how electrolysis works. |
