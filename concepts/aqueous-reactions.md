# Chapter 4 Concept Map: Reactions in Aqueous Solutions

## Source and Scope

**Reference:** instructor's own lecture notes (Yu Wang, University of Louisiana at Lafayette),
Chapter 4 (CC BY 4.0). **Audience:** introductory undergraduate general chemistry. **Note:** covers
General Properties of Aqueous Solutions, Precipitation Reactions, Acid-Base Reactions,
Oxidation-Reduction Reactions, and Solution Stoichiometry. Simple Markdown only — no images,
diagrams, or orz containers.

## Chapter Summary

Chapter 4 takes the reaction and mole concepts from Chapter 3 and situates them in the environment
where most general-chemistry reactions actually happen: water. It opens by classifying solutes as
strong, weak, or non-electrolytes based on how completely they ionize, which immediately explains
why some ionic compounds conduct electricity in solution and others do not. It then works through
three major reaction families in water — precipitation reactions (governed by solubility rules and
written as net ionic equations), acid-base reactions (Arrhenius and Brønsted definitions, strong vs.
weak acids/bases, and neutralization), and oxidation-reduction reactions (oxidation numbers and the
five classic redox reaction types, including the activity series for metal displacement).

The chapter closes by formalizing molarity and applying the mole-bridge method from Chapter 3 to
solutions specifically — solution stoichiometry, dilution, gravimetric analysis, and titration.
This chapter's three reaction families (precipitation, acid-base, redox) and its molarity-based
quantitative toolkit are both reused extensively: acid-base reactions become an entire two-chapter
topic later (Ch. 15–16), redox reactions become Ch. 18 Electrochemistry, and molarity is extended
to molality and mole fraction in Ch. 12.

### 1. General Properties of Aqueous Solutions (§4.1)

**Learning objectives:**
- **Objective 4.1a** — Classify solutes as strong, weak, or nonelectrolytes

**Concept flow:**
- Solution (solute + solvent) → electrolyte (conducts electricity when dissolved) vs.
  non-electrolyte
  - Electrolyte → strong (ionizes completely) vs. weak (ionizes partially)
    - Strong acids/bases and soluble salts → strong electrolytes; weak acids/bases → weak
      electrolytes

### 2. Precipitation Reactions (§4.2)

**Learning objectives:**
- **Objective 4.2a** — Apply solubility rules to predict whether a compound is soluble
- **Objective 4.2b** — Write molecular, ionic, and net ionic equations for precipitation reactions

**Concept flow:**
- Solubility rules (memorized exceptions and patterns) → predict precipitate formation
- Molecular equation → complete ionic equation (strong electrolytes shown as ions) → net ionic
  equation (spectator ions canceled)

### 3. Acid-Base Reactions (§4.3)

**Learning objectives:**
- **Objective 4.3a** — Distinguish Arrhenius and Brønsted acid/base definitions, and identify common strong and weak acids
- **Objective 4.3b** — Write net ionic equations for neutralization reactions

**Concept flow:**
- Arrhenius acid/base (produces H⁺/OH⁻ in water) → Brønsted acid/base (proton donor/acceptor), a
  more general definition
- Strong acids/bases (ionize completely, memorized list) vs. weak acids/bases (ionize partially)
- Neutralization (acid + base → salt + water) → net ionic form differs depending on whether the
  acid/base is strong or weak

### 4. Oxidation-Reduction Reactions (§4.4)

**Learning objectives:**
- **Objective 4.4a** — Determine oxidation numbers
- **Objective 4.4b** — Classify types of redox reactions and use the activity series

**Concept flow:**
- Redox reaction → oxidation (electron loss, oxidation number increases) + reduction (electron
  gain, oxidation number decreases), always paired
- Oxidation-number rules → assign a number to every atom in a compound or ion
- Reaction type classification → combination, decomposition, combustion, displacement,
  disproportionation
  - Displacement reactions → activity series ranks which metals/halogens will displace others

### 5. Solution Stoichiometry (§4.5)

**Learning objectives:**
- **Objective 4.5a** — Calculate molarity and apply the dilution equation
- **Objective 4.5b** — Apply solution stoichiometry (including ion concentration from a compound's molarity)

**Concept flow:**
- Molarity (mol solute / L solution) → the standard concentration unit for solution-phase
  reactions
- Dilution → moles of solute unchanged, so MᵢVᵢ = M_fV_f
- Gravimetric analysis (dissolve → react to precipitate → filter/dry/weigh → back-calculate
  unknown) and titration (add known-concentration solution until reaction is complete at the
  equivalence point, tracked with an indicator) → two experimental applications of solution
  stoichiometry

## Common Student Bottlenecks

| Bottleneck | Conceptual repair |
|---|---|
| Forgetting to cancel spectator ions when writing a net ionic equation | Require students to write out the complete ionic equation first, then explicitly circle and cancel any ion that appears unchanged on both sides before calling the equation "net." |
| Confusing "soluble" with "strong electrolyte" (a soluble compound is not automatically a strong electrolyte if it's a weak acid/base) | Separate the two questions explicitly: first ask "does it dissolve?" (solubility rules), then ask "does it ionize completely?" (strong vs. weak electrolyte). |
| Misassigning oxidation numbers when hydrogen or oxygen is in an exception case (metal hydrides, peroxides) | Teach the exceptions as a short, explicitly flagged checklist to consult before applying the default H = +1 / O = −2 rule. |
| Confusing the direction of metal displacement (which metal is "more active") | Anchor to the activity series table itself — the reaction proceeds only if the free (uncombined) metal is higher on the list than the metal ion it is displacing. |
| Applying MᵢVᵢ = M_fV_f to a reaction stoichiometry problem (rather than a pure dilution) | Ask first whether moles of solute are conserved (dilution) or consumed/produced in a reaction (stoichiometry) — the dilution formula applies only to the former. |
