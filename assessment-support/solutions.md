# Chapter 12 Assessment Guide: Physical Properties of Solutions

## Source and Format

**Reference:** Yu Wang's General Chemistry Lecture Notes, Chapter 12 (CC BY 4.0). **Audience:** introductory undergraduate general chemistry, second semester. **Note:** this is design guidance for building assessments — question *guides* an instructor or generator can instantiate into many concrete questions — not a finished question bank or answer key.

## General Assessment Priorities

- Prioritize **"why," not just "how much"** for Section 12.1 — a student who can calculate molality but cannot explain why a given solute dissolves (or doesn't) in a given solvent has missed the conceptual core of the chapter.
- For Section 12.2, test the **conversion bridges** (mass ↔ moles via molar mass; mass of solution ↔ volume via density) as explicit, reusable steps, not a single memorized formula per unit.
- For Section 12.3, always require students to **identify which concentration unit** a formula needs (molality for $K_b$/$K_f$, molarity for $\pi$) before plugging in numbers — this is the chapter's most common bottleneck.
- Include at least one electrolyte-solution question per relevant objective so the van't Hoff factor is never an afterthought.

## Objective 12.1a: Explain solution formation and classify solutions by saturation

### Target understanding

A student can classify a solution as saturated, unsaturated, or supersaturated, and can ==explain whether a given dissolution process should be exothermic or endothermic by comparing solute-solvent attraction to the solvent-solvent and solute-solute attractions it replaces==.

### Question guides

**1. Forward — classify a solution**
- Variables & ranges: a described solution (solute added, dissolves completely / partially / with visible undissolved excess).
- Constraint: correct classification requires reasoning about the solute's dissolution limit at the stated temperature, not just "it dissolved."
- Contexts: kitchen solutions (sugar in tea), lab preparations.
- Formats: short-answer or multiple-choice.
- Worked instantiation: "You add sugar to iced tea and see undissolved sugar sitting at the bottom no matter how long you stir. Is the solution saturated, unsaturated, or supersaturated?" → Saturated (it has reached its dissolution limit; the undissolved excess proves no more can dissolve at this temperature).

**2. Conceptual — exothermic vs. endothermic dissolution**
- Variables & ranges: a named dissolution process with an observed temperature change (solution warms or cools).
- Constraint: explanation must compare the three interactions (solvent-solvent, solute-solute, solute-solvent), not just restate the sign of $\Delta H$.
- Contexts: instant cold packs (endothermic), hand-warmer packs (exothermic).
- Formats: short-answer.
- Worked instantiation: "When $\ce{KNO3}$ dissolves in water, the solution becomes noticeably colder. Is this exothermic or endothermic? What does this suggest about the relative interaction strengths?" → Endothermic; solute-solvent attraction is weaker than the solvent-solvent and solute-solute attractions it replaces, so net energy is absorbed from the surroundings (the water), cooling it.

**3. Real-world / applied — supersaturation**
- Variables & ranges: a supersaturation scenario (rock candy, honey crystallizing, a hand-warmer pack).
- Constraint: explanation must describe how the supersaturated state was created and what triggers it to return to equilibrium.
- Contexts: cooking/candy-making, commercial heat packs.
- Formats: short-answer.
- Worked instantiation: "Rock candy forms when a hot, concentrated sugar solution cools slowly undisturbed. Why doesn't the excess sugar crystallize out immediately as the solution cools past saturation?" → Supersaturation requires no disturbance/nucleation site; without one, the excess solute stays dissolved in an unstable state until a trigger (a seed crystal, a scratch, a disturbance) initiates crystallization.

**4. Error analysis**
- Variables & ranges: a flawed claim about ideal solutions or the definition of solubility.
- Constraint: correction must restate the precise definition being confused.
- Contexts: common mix-ups between "concentrated" and "saturated."
- Formats: short-answer.
- Worked instantiation: "A student says a concentrated solution and a saturated solution are the same thing. Explain the error." → A solution can be concentrated (a lot of solute per volume) without being saturated (at its dissolution limit) — saturation is about the *limit*, not the *amount* relative to some everyday sense of "a lot."

### Design guidance by purpose

| Purpose | Cognitive level | How to design the task |
|---|---|---|
| Homework | Understand | Classify 3–4 described solutions by saturation state. |
| Group discussion | Analyze | Debate why supersaturated solutions are described as "unstable" rather than "impossible." |
| Quiz | Understand | One classification item, one exo/endo explanation item. |
| Exam | Analyze | Require the three-interaction comparison explicitly, not just the final sign of $\Delta H$. |
| Project/activity | Create | Grow rock candy (or a similar supersaturation demo) and write up the saturation states observed at each stage. |

## Objective 12.1b: Apply "like dissolves like" to predict solubility

### Target understanding

Given a solute and a choice of solvents, a student can predict relative solubility by comparing polarity/ionic character, correctly applying nonpolar-nonpolar, polar-polar, and ionic-polar compatibility.

### Question guides

**1. Forward — predict more-soluble solvent**
- Variables & ranges: a solute (polar, nonpolar, or ionic) and two candidate solvents (one polar, one nonpolar).
- Constraint: prediction must be justified by the specific IMF match, not just asserted.
- Contexts: cooking oils, cleaning solvents, ionic salts.
- Formats: short-answer or multiple-choice.
- Worked instantiation: see the study guide's worked example (vegetable oil/heptane, isopropyl alcohol/water, KBr/water).

**2. Comparison / ranking**
- Variables & ranges: 3–4 solutes with a range of polarities, ranked by expected solubility in a single named solvent.
- Constraint: ranking must follow from a stated polarity/IMF comparison for each solute.
- Contexts: a homologous series of alcohols (increasing nonpolar chain length) in water.
- Formats: workout with justification.
- Worked instantiation: "Rank methanol, propanol, and octanol by expected solubility in water, from most to least soluble." → Methanol > propanol > octanol — as the nonpolar hydrocarbon chain lengthens, the molecule becomes progressively less water-compatible despite retaining one polar –OH group.

**3. Conceptual — miscibility**
- Variables & ranges: two named liquids, asked whether they are miscible.
- Constraint: explanation must reference relative polarity, not just a yes/no guess.
- Contexts: oil and vinegar (salad dressing), rubbing alcohol and water.
- Formats: short-answer.
- Worked instantiation: "Why do oil and vinegar separate into layers instead of forming one solution?" → Oil is nonpolar and vinegar (mostly water) is polar; their intermolecular forces are incompatible, so they are not miscible.

**4. Real-world / applied**
- Variables & ranges: a product-design or cleaning scenario requiring solvent choice.
- Constraint: choice must be justified by matching solvent polarity to the target solute.
- Contexts: choosing a stain remover, a paint thinner, a supplement's delivery solvent.
- Formats: short-answer.
- Worked instantiation: "A grease stain (nonpolar) won't come out with plain water. Why does a nonpolar solvent (like a dry-cleaning solvent) work better?" → Grease is nonpolar; a nonpolar solvent's dispersion forces are compatible with grease's, letting it dissolve, while water's polar forces are not.

### Design guidance by purpose

| Purpose | Cognitive level | How to design the task |
|---|---|---|
| Homework | Apply | 4–5 solute/solvent pairs, predict more-soluble solvent and justify. |
| Group discussion | Analyze | Sort a mixed set of solute cards by "best solvent" and defend borderline cases. |
| Quiz | Apply | One prediction item, one ranking item. |
| Exam | Analyze | Combine with a structure (given a Lewis structure, judge polarity first, then predict solubility). |
| Project/activity | Evaluate | Test (or research) real solubility data for a set of household solutes and explain any surprises. |

## Objective 12.1c: Predict temperature/pressure effects on solubility, including Henry's law

### Target understanding

A student can state that gas solubility always decreases with increasing temperature (unlike solids, which have no universal rule), that pressure has no effect on solid/liquid solubility, and can apply Henry's law to a gas that does not react with its solvent — correctly identifying when Henry's law does *not* apply.

### Question guides

**1. Forward — Henry's law proportionality**
- Variables & ranges: a gas's solubility at one pressure, asked to predict solubility at a different pressure (same temperature, non-reactive gas).
- Constraint: student must apply direct proportionality, not a different relationship.
- Contexts: carbonated beverages, dissolved oxygen in water.
- Formats: workout.
- Worked instantiation: "If $\ce{O2}$ solubility in water is 0.020 M at 1.0 atm, estimate its solubility at 0.5 atm." → Solubility is directly proportional to pressure: $0.020\ \text{M}\times 0.5 = 0.010\ \text{M}$.

**2. Conceptual — Henry's law exception**
- Variables & ranges: a gas that reacts with water (CO₂, NH₃) vs. one that doesn't (O₂, N₂).
- Constraint: explanation must state the reacting case's actual complication, not just "it's different."
- Contexts: soda carbonation, ammonia solutions.
- Formats: short-answer.
- Worked instantiation: "Why can't Henry's law be used to precisely predict how much $\ce{CO2}$ dissolves in water at a given pressure?" → $\ce{CO2}$ partially reacts with water to form carbonic acid, so the simple proportionality (which assumes no chemical reaction) does not hold exactly.

**3. Predict-the-trend — temperature effect**
- Variables & ranges: a solid solute vs. a gas solute, same temperature increase.
- Constraint: student must state the gas case as a universal rule and the solid case as "cannot be determined without more information."
- Contexts: comparing sugar dissolving vs. a carbonated drink going flat when warmed.
- Formats: short-answer.
- Worked instantiation: "A glass of iced tea with undissolved sugar is left to warm to room temperature. A sealed bottle of soda is also warmed. Predict what happens to solubility in each case." → The sugar's solubility may increase or decrease — no universal rule for solids. The dissolved $\ce{CO2}$'s solubility definitely decreases — gas solubility always drops as temperature rises.

**4. Real-world / applied**
- Variables & ranges: a diving/altitude/carbonation scenario involving pressure change.
- Constraint: answer must connect the pressure change to Henry's law directly.
- Contexts: scuba diving (nitrogen), opening a soda bottle.
- Formats: short-answer.
- Worked instantiation: "Why does a soda fizz when you open the bottle?" → Opening the bottle drops the pressure above the liquid; by Henry's law, lower pressure means lower $\ce{CO2}$ solubility, so dissolved $\ce{CO2}$ rapidly leaves solution as gas bubbles.

### Design guidance by purpose

| Purpose | Cognitive level | How to design the task |
|---|---|---|
| Homework | Apply | 2–3 Henry's law proportionality calculations, one exception-case explanation. |
| Group discussion | Understand | Discuss why the textbook says "no universal rule" for solids but a firm rule for gases. |
| Quiz | Understand/Apply | One Henry's law calculation, one true/false-with-justification on the reacting-gas exception. |
| Exam | Analyze | Combine a Henry's law calculation with a conceptual explanation of why it would fail for a reactive gas. |
| Project/activity | Evaluate | Research a real diving/altitude-sickness scenario and explain it via Henry's law. |

## Objective 12.2a: Calculate mole fraction, percent by mass, molarity, and molality

### Target understanding

A student can compute any of the four concentration units directly from given masses/volumes/moles, correctly identifying which quantities belong in the numerator and denominator for each.

### Question guides

**1. Forward — compute a single unit**
- Variables & ranges: a mass of solute (0.1–50 g) and mass or volume of solvent/solution.
- Constraint: student must select the correct denominator (total moles, mass of solution, volume of solution, or mass of solvent) for the unit requested.
- Contexts: any common solute/solvent pair.
- Formats: workout.
- Worked instantiation: see the study guide's worked example (0.850 g $\ce{NH3}$ in 125 g water → mole fraction and molality).

**2. Multi-step — compute all four from one scenario**
- Variables & ranges: a single solution scenario with enough data (mass of solute, mass or volume of solution, density) to compute all four units.
- Constraint: student must track units carefully across four different calculations from the same starting data.
- Contexts: a concentrated acid or base solution (with density given).
- Formats: workout.
- Worked instantiation: given mass of solute, mass of solvent, and density, compute mole fraction, percent by mass, molarity, and molality in sequence.

**3. Inverse — solve for an unknown mass or volume**
- Variables & ranges: given a target concentration and total solution amount, solve for the mass of solute needed.
- Constraint: correct algebraic rearrangement of the concentration formula.
- Contexts: preparing a solution to a specification (a recipe, a lab prep).
- Formats: workout.
- Worked instantiation: "How many grams of NaCl are needed to prepare 500 mL of a 0.150 M solution?" → Rearrange $M = n/V$ to find moles, then convert to grams via molar mass.

**4. Conceptual — unit selection**
- Variables & ranges: a scenario describing what's held constant (a fixed mass of solvent vs. a fixed volume of solution).
- Constraint: answer must justify which unit is most natural for the described scenario.
- Contexts: lab prep instructions.
- Formats: short-answer.
- Worked instantiation: "A recipe for a buffer says to dissolve a fixed mass of solute per kilogram of water. Which concentration unit does this describe, and why is it convenient here?" → Molality — because it is defined per mass of solvent, it is easy to prepare by weighing rather than measuring a final solution volume.

### Design guidance by purpose

| Purpose | Cognitive level | How to design the task |
|---|---|---|
| Homework | Apply | Compute 2–3 different units from the same scenario. |
| Group discussion | Understand | Discuss which unit is most convenient for a given real lab task. |
| Quiz | Apply | One direct calculation of a named unit. |
| Exam | Analyze | Require all four units from one dataset, in sequence. |
| Project/activity | Evaluate | Prepare (or describe preparing) a solution to a target concentration and verify the calculation. |

## Objective 12.2b: Convert between concentration units given density and molar mass

### Target understanding

A student can convert from one concentration unit to another (e.g., molality to molarity, or mole fraction to molality) using density and molar mass as the bridge, correctly choosing a convenient reference amount to simplify the calculation.

### Question guides

**1. Forward — molality to molarity**
- Variables & ranges: a molality and a density (1.0–1.5 g/mL range), find molarity.
- Constraint: student must choose a convenient basis (e.g., "assume 1 kg of solvent") and carry it through consistently.
- Contexts: commercial concentrated acid/base solutions.
- Formats: workout.
- Worked instantiation: see the study guide's worked example (33.7 m $\ce{HNO3}$, density 1.35 g/mL → 14.6 M).

**2. Inverse — molarity to molality**
- Variables & ranges: a molarity and density, find molality.
- Constraint: same bridge, applied in reverse (basis: "assume 1 L of solution").
- Contexts: laboratory-grade reagent bottles (which list molarity or percent by mass, not molality).
- Formats: workout.
- Worked instantiation: given a reagent's molarity and density, find its molality.

**3. Multi-step — mole fraction to molarity**
- Variables & ranges: given mole fraction and density, find molarity (routes through both bridges).
- Constraint: requires converting mole fraction to a mass basis first, then applying density.
- Contexts: concentrated laboratory acids specified by percent or mole fraction on the label.
- Formats: workout.
- Worked instantiation: given mole fraction of a solute and the solution's density, find molarity, showing every intermediate step.

**4. Error analysis**
- Variables & ranges: a flawed conversion that skips the density bridge entirely (treating grams of solution as if it were volume in mL directly).
- Constraint: correction must identify exactly where density was needed and wasn't used.
- Contexts: a common shortcut-taking mistake.
- Formats: short-answer.
- Worked instantiation: "A student converts 2.12 kg of solution directly to 2.12 L, skipping density. What's wrong?" → Mass and volume are only interchangeable through density (volume = mass ÷ density); assuming 1 kg = 1 L is only valid if density is exactly 1.00 kg/L, which is rarely true for a concentrated solution.

### Design guidance by purpose

| Purpose | Cognitive level | How to design the task |
|---|---|---|
| Homework | Apply | 2–3 unit-conversion problems, each starting from a different unit. |
| Group discussion | Analyze | Walk through choosing a convenient reference basis for a conversion together. |
| Quiz | Apply | One direct molality↔molarity conversion. |
| Exam | Analyze | Require a multi-step conversion touching three of the four units. |
| Project/activity | Evaluate | Look up a real reagent bottle's label concentration and convert it to a different unit. |

## Objective 12.3a: Apply Raoult's law to calculate vapor-pressure lowering

### Target understanding

A student can calculate a solution's vapor pressure from the pure solvent's vapor pressure and the solvent's mole fraction, and can explain why vapor pressure is always lower for a solution than for the pure solvent (given a nonvolatile solute).

### Question guides

**1. Forward — calculate solution vapor pressure**
- Variables & ranges: a solute mass, solvent mass, and pure-solvent vapor pressure.
- Constraint: student must compute the *solvent's* mole fraction (not the solute's) before applying Raoult's law.
- Contexts: aqueous solutions of common nonvolatile solutes (sugar, urea, salts).
- Formats: workout.
- Worked instantiation: see the study guide's worked example (5.00 g urea in 100 g water, $P^0$ = 23.7 torr → 23.4 torr).

**2. Inverse — solve for solute moles from observed vapor-pressure lowering**
- Variables & ranges: given the vapor-pressure lowering and pure-solvent vapor pressure, find moles of solute.
- Constraint: requires rearranging Raoult's law to isolate mole fraction, then moles.
- Contexts: identifying an unknown solute's molar mass from vapor-pressure data.
- Formats: workout.
- Worked instantiation: given observed $P$ and $P^0$, back-calculate $X_\text{solute}$ and then moles of solute for a known solvent mass.

**3. Conceptual — why vapor pressure drops**
- Variables & ranges: a qualitative "explain why" prompt.
- Constraint: explanation must reference solvent mole fraction/surface coverage, not a vague "solute gets in the way."
- Contexts: any nonvolatile-solute solution.
- Formats: short-answer.
- Worked instantiation: "Explain, using Raoult's law, why adding a nonvolatile solute always lowers a solvent's vapor pressure." → Because $P_1 = X_1P_1^0$ and $X_1 < 1$ whenever any solute is present (mole fraction of solvent must be less than 1), $P_1$ must be less than $P_1^0$.

**4. Multi-step / synthesis**
- Variables & ranges: combine Raoult's law with a molar-mass-determination scenario (Objective 12.2a).
- Constraint: correctly sequences mole-fraction calculation with mass/molar-mass reasoning.
- Contexts: determining an unknown compound's identity from its effect on vapor pressure.
- Formats: workout.
- Worked instantiation: given the mass of an unknown nonvolatile solute, solvent mass, and observed vapor-pressure lowering, determine the solute's approximate molar mass.

### Design guidance by purpose

| Purpose | Cognitive level | How to design the task |
|---|---|---|
| Homework | Apply | 2–3 forward vapor-pressure calculations. |
| Group discussion | Understand | Discuss why only *nonvolatile* solutes are assumed in these problems. |
| Quiz | Apply | One forward Raoult's law calculation. |
| Exam | Analyze | Require solving for an unknown molar mass from vapor-pressure data. |
| Project/activity | Evaluate | Research a real application of vapor-pressure lowering (e.g., antifreeze). |

## Objective 12.3b: Calculate boiling-point elevation and freezing-point depression

### Target understanding

A student can calculate $\Delta T_b$ or $\Delta T_f$ from molality and the solvent's $K_b$/$K_f$, correctly using molality (not molarity), and can state the new boiling or freezing point.

### Question guides

**1. Forward — calculate $\Delta T_b$ or $\Delta T_f$**
- Variables & ranges: a solute mass, solvent mass, and $K_b$ or $K_f$ value (provided).
- Constraint: student must convert to molality (not molarity) before applying the formula.
- Contexts: aqueous and non-aqueous solvents (benzene, water).
- Formats: workout.
- Worked instantiation: see the study guide's worked example (1.0 g glycerin in 47.8 g water, $K_b$ = 0.512 °C/m → 100.12 °C).

**2. Inverse — solve for molar mass from $\Delta T_f$**
- Variables & ranges: given an observed $\Delta T_f$, solute mass, solvent mass, and $K_f$, find the solute's molar mass.
- Constraint: requires solving for molality first, then working backward to moles and molar mass.
- Contexts: identifying an unknown nonelectrolyte solute.
- Formats: workout.
- Worked instantiation: given $\Delta T_f$, mass of an unknown solute, mass of solvent, and $K_f$, determine the solute's approximate molar mass.

**3. Comparison / ranking — electrolyte vs. nonelectrolyte**
- Variables & ranges: two solutions of equal molality, one electrolyte and one nonelectrolyte.
- Constraint: must invoke the van't Hoff factor to justify the ranking (links to Objective 12.3d).
- Contexts: table sugar vs. table salt solutions.
- Formats: short-answer.
- Worked instantiation: "Two water solutions have the same molality (0.02 mol/kg): one is table sugar, the other table salt. Which has the higher boiling point? Why?" → The table salt (NaCl) solution — it dissociates into 2 ions per formula unit ($i \approx 2$), roughly doubling its effective particle concentration compared to sugar's ($i = 1$).

**4. Real-world / applied**
- Variables & ranges: an everyday freezing-point-depression or boiling-point-elevation scenario.
- Constraint: answer must connect the practical outcome to the molality-proportional formula.
- Contexts: antifreeze in a car radiator, salting a driveway.
- Formats: short-answer.
- Worked instantiation: "Why does adding antifreeze to a car's radiator both raise the boiling point and lower the freezing point of the coolant?" → Antifreeze acts as a solute in water; its presence simultaneously produces $\Delta T_b$ (raises boiling point) and $\Delta T_f$ (lowers freezing point), both proportional to its molality.

### Design guidance by purpose

| Purpose | Cognitive level | How to design the task |
|---|---|---|
| Homework | Apply | 2–3 forward $\Delta T_b$/$\Delta T_f$ calculations. |
| Group discussion | Analyze | Compare electrolyte vs. nonelectrolyte solutions at equal molality. |
| Quiz | Apply | One forward calculation, explicitly requiring the molality conversion step. |
| Exam | Analyze | Require solving for an unknown molar mass from freezing-point data. |
| Project/activity | Evaluate | Research real antifreeze/de-icing formulations and explain the chemistry. |

## Objective 12.3c: Calculate osmotic pressure

### Target understanding

A student can calculate osmotic pressure from molarity, temperature, and $R$, and can explain semipermeable membranes and the direction of net solvent flow during osmosis.

### Question guides

**1. Forward — calculate $\pi$**
- Variables & ranges: solute mass, solution volume, temperature.
- Constraint: student must use molarity (not molality) — the one colligative property that does.
- Contexts: biological solutions (blood, cell fluid), lab-prepared solutions.
- Formats: workout.
- Worked instantiation: see the study guide's worked example (5.0 g methanol in 0.750 L at 37 °C → 5.3 atm).

**2. Inverse — determine molar mass from $\pi$**
- Variables & ranges: given observed osmotic pressure, solute mass, solution volume, and temperature, find molar mass.
- Constraint: requires solving $\pi = MRT$ for $M$, then converting to moles and molar mass — the standard method for determining large-molecule (e.g., protein) molar masses, since $\pi$ is measurably large even for very dilute solutions.
- Contexts: determining the molar mass of a protein or polymer.
- Formats: workout.
- Worked instantiation: given the osmotic pressure of a dilute protein solution, its mass, volume, and temperature, determine the protein's approximate molar mass.

**3. Conceptual — direction of osmosis**
- Variables & ranges: two solutions of different concentration separated by a semipermeable membrane.
- Constraint: explanation must correctly state the direction of *net solvent* flow (toward the more concentrated side), not solute flow.
- Contexts: cells in solutions of different concentration (biology connection).
- Formats: short-answer.
- Worked instantiation: "A cell is placed in a solution more concentrated than its own interior. Which way does water flow, and what happens to the cell?" → Water flows out of the cell (toward the more concentrated external solution), and the cell shrinks (crenates).

**4. Real-world / applied**
- Variables & ranges: a real osmotic-pressure application (reverse osmosis, IV fluids, food preservation).
- Constraint: answer must connect the application to the direction/magnitude of osmotic pressure.
- Contexts: desalination, medical saline solutions, salting food to preserve it.
- Formats: short-answer.
- Worked instantiation: "Why does salting meat help preserve it?" → The high external salt concentration creates a strong osmotic gradient that draws water out of any bacterial cells present, dehydrating and inhibiting them.

### Design guidance by purpose

| Purpose | Cognitive level | How to design the task |
|---|---|---|
| Homework | Apply | 2–3 forward osmotic-pressure calculations. |
| Group discussion | Understand | Discuss why osmotic pressure (not freezing-point depression) is preferred for determining large-molecule molar masses. |
| Quiz | Apply | One forward calculation. |
| Exam | Analyze | Require solving for an unknown molar mass from osmotic-pressure data. |
| Project/activity | Evaluate | Research a real reverse-osmosis (desalination) system and explain the chemistry. |

## Objective 12.3d: Apply the van't Hoff factor to electrolyte solutions

### Target understanding

A student can determine the van't Hoff factor from a solute's dissociation equation and apply it correctly to any of the three colligative-property formulas, without defaulting to $i = 1$ for an ionic compound.

### Question guides

**1. Forward — determine $i$ and apply it**
- Variables & ranges: a named electrolyte (NaCl, CaCl₂, $\ce{K2SO4}$, etc.) and a molality.
- Constraint: student must write the dissociation equation to determine $i$ before calculating.
- Contexts: common salts in aqueous solution.
- Formats: workout.
- Worked instantiation: "Calculate $\Delta T_f$ for a 0.10 m $\ce{CaCl2}$ solution in water ($K_f$ = 1.86 °C/m)." → $\ce{CaCl2} \rightarrow \ce{Ca^2+} + 2\ce{Cl-}$, so $i = 3$: $\Delta T_f = 3\times 1.86\times 0.10 = 0.558\ \text{°C}$.

**2. Comparison / ranking — multiple electrolytes**
- Variables & ranges: 3–4 solutes with different van't Hoff factors, same molality.
- Constraint: ranking must be justified by each solute's dissociation equation.
- Contexts: comparing NaCl, $\ce{CaCl2}$, $\ce{Na3PO4}$, and glucose at equal molality.
- Formats: workout.
- Worked instantiation: rank glucose ($i=1$), NaCl ($i=2$), and $\ce{Na3PO4}$ ($i=4$) by expected freezing-point depression at equal molality.

**3. Inverse — determine $i$ from experimental data**
- Variables & ranges: given an observed $\Delta T_f$ or $\pi$ that doesn't match the "ideal" $i$, ask students to calculate the experimental $i$.
- Constraint: introduces the idea that real electrolytes may show an $i$ slightly less than the ideal integer value (ion pairing), without requiring deep treatment.
- Contexts: real (non-ideal) electrolyte behavior.
- Formats: workout (optional/advanced).
- Worked instantiation: given an observed $\Delta T_f$ for a NaCl solution slightly less than $i=2$ would predict, calculate the experimental $i$ and note it is less than 2.

**4. Error analysis**
- Variables & ranges: a flawed calculation using $i = 1$ for an obviously ionic solute.
- Constraint: correction must identify the missing dissociation step.
- Contexts: the exact bottleneck flagged in the concept map.
- Formats: short-answer.
- Worked instantiation: "A student calculates the osmotic pressure of a NaCl solution using $\pi = MRT$ directly, without a van't Hoff factor. What's missing, and how does it change the answer?" → NaCl dissociates into 2 ions, so $i = 2$ is missing; the correct formula is $\pi = iMRT$, giving double the naive answer.

### Design guidance by purpose

| Purpose | Cognitive level | How to design the task |
|---|---|---|
| Homework | Apply | 3–4 colligative-property calculations across different electrolytes, requiring $i$ each time. |
| Group discussion | Analyze | Debate why real $i$ values are sometimes slightly less than the ideal integer (ion pairing, for stronger students). |
| Quiz | Apply | One calculation requiring the student to determine $i$ from a formula first. |
| Exam | Analyze | Rank multiple electrolyte solutions by a colligative property at equal molality. |
| Project/activity | Evaluate | Research real deviations from ideal $i$ values and what causes them. |

## Rubric Themes for Chapter 12

| Evidence of mastery | What to look for |
|---|---|
| Solubility predictions justified by IMF comparison | Student names the specific force type (polar/nonpolar/ionic match), not just "similar" or "different." |
| Correct unit selection before calculation | Student identifies which concentration unit a formula requires (molality for $K_b$/$K_f$, molarity for $\pi$) before substituting numbers. |
| Explicit conversion bridges shown | Mass↔mole (via molar mass) and mass↔volume (via density) steps are shown explicitly, not skipped. |
| Van't Hoff factor never defaulted to 1 | For any ionic solute, the dissociation equation is written and $i$ is derived from it, every time. |
| Correct direction/magnitude reasoning for colligative properties | Vapor pressure is lower, boiling point is higher, freezing point is lower, and osmotic pressure opposes concentrated-side dilution — each stated in the correct direction, not just calculated numerically. |
| Henry's law exception recognized | Student flags CO₂/NH₃-type reactive gases as exceptions to Henry's law rather than applying it uncritically. |
