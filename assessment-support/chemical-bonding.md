# Chapter 9 Assessment Guide: Chemical Bonding — Basic Concepts

## Source and Format

**Reference:** Yu Wang's General Chemistry Lecture Notes, Chapter 9 (CC BY 4.0). **Audience:** introductory undergraduate general chemistry, first semester. **Note:** this is design guidance for building assessments — question *guides* an instructor or generator can instantiate into many concrete questions — not a finished question bank or answer key.

## General Assessment Priorities

- Require electronegativity-difference reasoning (not memorized "this compound is ionic") for every bond-classification question — the whole point of this section is a quantitative, generalizable rule.
- Require the full valence-electron count as an explicit first step in every Lewis-structure question, since miscounting there cascades into every later step.
- Test formal charge on multiple atoms within the same structure in one question, so students practice the "sum to overall charge" check.
- Keep bond-enthalpy problems anchored to real reactions (not abstract "bond A breaks, bond B forms" statements) so the sign convention has a physical anchor.

## Objective 9.1a: Draw Lewis dot symbols and explain ionic bonding and lattice energy

### Target understanding

A student can draw a correct Lewis dot symbol for a representative element or monatomic ion, and can explain what determines an ionic compound's lattice energy.

### Question guides

**1. Forward — draw a Lewis dot symbol**
- Variables & ranges: a representative element or a common monatomic ion.
- Constraint: correct dot count (matching valence electrons/charge).
- Contexts: any Group 1A–7A element or ion.
- Formats: workout.
- Worked instantiation: see the practice sheet (chloride ion Lewis dot symbol).

**2. Inverse — rank lattice energies**
- Variables & ranges: 2–3 ionic compounds with different ion charges and/or sizes.
- Constraint: must justify using both charge and size reasoning.
- Contexts: compounds like NaCl, MgO, MgCl₂.
- Formats: short-answer.
- Worked instantiation: "Rank NaCl, MgO, and MgCl₂ by expected lattice energy." → MgO (two doubly-charged ions) > MgCl₂ (one doubly-charged ion) > NaCl (two singly-charged ions).

**3. Conceptual — why lattice energy correlates with melting point**
- Variables & ranges: qualitative.
- Constraint: must connect lattice energy to the energy needed to disrupt the solid.
- Contexts: any ionic solid.
- Formats: short-answer.
- Worked instantiation: see the study guide's lattice-energy/melting-point correlation statement.

**4. Error analysis**
- Variables & ranges: a student assumes ion size doesn't matter, only charge.
- Constraint: correction must state the size effect explicitly.
- Contexts: comparing two same-charge, different-size ion pairs.
- Formats: short-answer.
- Worked instantiation: "A student says only ion charge affects lattice energy, not size. What's missing?" → Smaller ions can approach each other more closely, producing stronger coulombic attraction and higher lattice energy, even at the same charge — ion size matters independently of charge.

### Design guidance by purpose

| Purpose | Cognitive level | How to design the task |
|---|---|---|
| Homework | Apply | 3–4 Lewis-dot-symbol and lattice-energy-ranking items. |
| Group discussion | Understand | Discuss why lattice energy can't be measured directly (it requires a Born-Haber-cycle-style approach, beyond this course's scope). |
| Quiz | Apply | One Lewis dot symbol or lattice-energy-ranking item. |
| Exam | Analyze | Combine with Objective 9.1b (classify a compound as ionic, then discuss its lattice energy). |
| Project/activity | Understand | Students rank 4 real ionic compounds' known melting points and check against predicted lattice-energy ranking. |

## Objective 9.1b: Classify bond type using electronegativity difference

### Target understanding

A student can calculate or estimate an electronegativity difference and classify the resulting bond as ionic, polar covalent, or (nonpolar) covalent.

### Question guides

**1. Forward — classify a bond from given electronegativities**
- Variables & ranges: two elements with known Pauling electronegativities.
- Constraint: correct application of the 2.0/0.3 cutoffs.
- Contexts: any element pair.
- Formats: workout.
- Worked instantiation: see the practice sheet (identifying the polar covalent bond among 5 pairs).

**2. Inverse — predict which of two bonds is more polar**
- Variables & ranges: two different bonding pairs.
- Constraint: must compare $\Delta\text{EN}$ values directly, not just intuition.
- Contexts: any two bond pairs.
- Formats: short-answer.
- Worked instantiation: see the practice sheet (greatest % ionic character among H–C, H–S, H–O, H–N).

**3. Conceptual — why the 2.0/0.3 cutoffs are approximate**
- Variables & ranges: qualitative.
- Constraint: must acknowledge these are useful guidelines, not sharp physical boundaries.
- Contexts: a borderline case near one of the cutoffs.
- Formats: short-answer.
- Worked instantiation: "The study guide notes the $\Delta\text{EN}\ge2.0$ ionic rule 'applies to most but not all' compounds. Why might a real compound not follow this cutoff cleanly?" → Bond character exists on a continuous spectrum from fully covalent to fully ionic; the numeric cutoffs are a useful teaching approximation, not a sharp physical threshold, so some real compounds fall on the "wrong" side of the boundary despite genuinely being mostly ionic or mostly covalent.

**4. Error analysis**
- Variables & ranges: a student confuses electronegativity with electron affinity.
- Constraint: correction must state the specific distinction.
- Contexts: any bond-polarity discussion.
- Formats: short-answer.
- Worked instantiation: "A student says electronegativity and electron affinity are the same thing. What's the distinction?" → Electron affinity describes an *isolated* atom's attraction for an additional electron; electronegativity describes an atom's pull on *shared bonding electrons* while already bonded to another atom — related concepts, but not identical.

### Design guidance by purpose

| Purpose | Cognitive level | How to design the task |
|---|---|---|
| Homework | Apply | 4–6 bond-classification items using electronegativity data. |
| Group discussion | Understand | Discuss why the same two elements can form bonds classified differently in different specific contexts. |
| Quiz | Apply | One bond-classification item. |
| Exam | Analyze | Combine with Objective 8's electronegativity trend content (identify likely-high/low EN elements first, then classify their bond). |
| Project/activity | Apply | Students classify all the bonds in 3 real compounds using a Pauling electronegativity table. |

## Objective 9.2a: Draw Lewis structures using the standard procedure

### Target understanding

A student can draw a correct Lewis structure for a molecule or polyatomic ion, following the 4-step procedure and correctly accounting for ion charge in the electron count.

### Question guides

**1. Forward — draw a neutral molecule's Lewis structure**
- Variables & ranges: any simple covalent molecule.
- Constraint: correct total valence-electron count as step 1.
- Contexts: any molecule.
- Formats: workout.
- Worked instantiation: see the practice sheet (NH₃, F₂, N₂, CH₄).

**2. Inverse — draw a polyatomic ion's Lewis structure**
- Variables & ranges: a common polyatomic cation or anion.
- Constraint: correct electron-count adjustment for charge (add for anion, subtract for cation).
- Contexts: any polyatomic ion.
- Formats: workout.
- Worked instantiation: see the practice sheet ($\ce{H3O+}$).

**3. Conceptual — why multiple bonds are needed for some structures**
- Variables & ranges: qualitative, referencing step 4 of the procedure.
- Constraint: must connect to an incomplete central-atom octet after step 3.
- Contexts: any structure requiring a double or triple bond.
- Formats: short-answer.
- Worked instantiation: "Why does $\ce{CO2}$'s Lewis structure need double bonds instead of all single bonds?" → With only single bonds, the central carbon would have just 4 electrons around it (2 bonds × 2 electrons ÷ 2... ) — short of an octet; converting lone pairs on each oxygen into a second bond to carbon completes carbon's octet using the same total electron count.

**4. Error analysis**
- Variables & ranges: a student miscounts total valence electrons for a polyatomic ion by forgetting the charge adjustment.
- Constraint: correction must show the missing add/subtract step.
- Contexts: any polyatomic ion.
- Formats: short-answer.
- Worked instantiation: "A student draws $\ce{H3O+}$ using only the neutral atoms' valence electrons (1+1+1+6=9), without adjusting for the +1 charge. What's wrong?" → For a cation, subtract the positive charge from the total: $9-1=8$ valence electrons should be used, not 9.

### Design guidance by purpose

| Purpose | Cognitive level | How to design the task |
|---|---|---|
| Homework | Apply | 4–6 Lewis-structure-drawing problems, including at least one ion. |
| Group discussion | Understand | Work through a structure requiring step 4 (multiple bonds) as a group. |
| Quiz | Apply | One Lewis-structure-drawing problem. |
| Exam | Analyze | Combine with Objective 9.2b (draw the structure, then compute formal charges on it). |
| Project/activity | Apply | Students draw Lewis structures for 5 molecules relevant to a real application (e.g., common industrial gases). |

## Objective 9.2b: Calculate formal charge

### Target understanding

A student can calculate the formal charge on any atom in a Lewis structure, and can use formal charge to judge which of several possible structures is most plausible.

### Question guides

**1. Forward — calculate formal charge on a specified atom**
- Variables & ranges: an atom within a given Lewis structure.
- Constraint: correct application of the formal-charge formula.
- Contexts: any Lewis structure.
- Formats: workout.
- Worked instantiation: see the practice sheet (formal charge on carbon for zero formal charge, and formal charge on O in N₂O).

**2. Inverse — determine bonding pattern from a target formal charge**
- Variables & ranges: a target formal charge and an atom's valence-electron count.
- Constraint: student must work backward to the required lone-pair/bonding-electron combination.
- Contexts: any atom.
- Formats: workout.
- Worked instantiation: see the practice sheet (how many covalent bonds give carbon a formal charge of zero).

**3. Conceptual — formal charge vs. actual charge**
- Variables & ranges: qualitative.
- Constraint: must state formal charge is a bookkeeping convention (electrons split evenly), not a measure of real charge distribution.
- Contexts: any polar bond.
- Formats: short-answer.
- Worked instantiation: "Does a formal charge of 0 mean an atom has no real partial charge in the molecule?" → Not necessarily — formal charge assumes bonding electrons are split exactly evenly regardless of electronegativity, which is a simplification; the atom's *actual* charge distribution also depends on electronegativity differences (captured better by oxidation number or a full polarity analysis), so a formal charge of 0 doesn't guarantee zero real partial charge.

**4. Error analysis**
- Variables & ranges: a student assigns all bonding electrons (not half) to one atom when computing formal charge.
- Constraint: correction must restate the "half of bonding electrons" rule.
- Contexts: any Lewis structure.
- Formats: short-answer.
- Worked instantiation: "A student computes formal charge using all 4 electrons in a double bond, not half. What's wrong?" → Formal charge assigns only *half* of the bonding electrons in any bond (single, double, or triple) to each atom — for a double bond (4 electrons total), each atom gets credit for 2, not 4.

### Design guidance by purpose

| Purpose | Cognitive level | How to design the task |
|---|---|---|
| Homework | Apply | 4–6 formal-charge calculations across different structures. |
| Group discussion | Analyze | Compare two candidate structures for the same molecule using formal charge to pick the more plausible one. |
| Quiz | Apply | One formal-charge calculation. |
| Exam | Analyze | Combine with Objective 9.2a (draw the structure, then evaluate it via formal charge). |
| Project/activity | Apply | Students compute formal charges for every atom in 3 polyatomic ions and verify the charges sum to the ion's overall charge. |

## Objective 9.2c: Identify and explain resonance structures

### Target understanding

A student can recognize when a molecule requires resonance structures, draw the alternative structures, and correctly explain what resonance does and does not mean physically.

### Question guides

**1. Forward — identify a molecule requiring resonance**
- Variables & ranges: a molecule with a delocalized bonding pattern (ozone, carbonate, benzene, nitrous oxide).
- Constraint: student must draw at least two valid resonance structures.
- Contexts: any classic resonance example.
- Formats: workout.
- Worked instantiation: see the study guide's ozone example.

**2. Inverse — explain why a single structure is insufficient**
- Variables & ranges: any resonance example.
- Constraint: must reference that real bond lengths/properties are intermediate between the individual structures.
- Contexts: ozone or carbonate.
- Formats: short-answer.
- Worked instantiation: "Why can't a single Lewis structure of ozone (one O=O double bond, one O-O single bond) accurately represent the real molecule?" → Experimentally, both O-O bonds in ozone are equal length, intermediate between a typical single and double bond — neither individual resonance structure alone matches this; only the resonance concept (both contributing equally) captures the actual, symmetric bonding.

**3. Conceptual — the common misconception about resonance**
- Variables & ranges: qualitative.
- Constraint: must explicitly correct the "rapidly flips between structures" misconception.
- Contexts: any resonance example.
- Formats: short-answer.
- Worked instantiation: see the study guide's explicit caution against the "shifts quickly back and forth" misconception.

**4. Error analysis**
- Variables & ranges: a student describes a resonance-stabilized molecule as literally oscillating between the drawn structures over time.
- Constraint: correction must restate that resonance structures are a human representational tool, not a physical process.
- Contexts: any resonance example.
- Formats: short-answer.
- Worked instantiation: see the study guide's Synthesis-adjacent caution (resonance is "a human invention," addressing limitations of simple bonding models — not a real back-and-forth switching process).

### Design guidance by purpose

| Purpose | Cognitive level | How to design the task |
|---|---|---|
| Homework | Apply | 2–3 resonance-structure-drawing problems. |
| Group discussion | Analyze | Debate the "does it flip back and forth" misconception directly, then resolve it together. |
| Quiz | Apply | One resonance-identification item. |
| Exam | Analyze | Combine with Objective 9.2b (compute formal charges across the different resonance structures of the same molecule). |
| Project/activity | Understand | Students research why benzene's resonance stabilization makes it less reactive than a hypothetical single-structure alternative would predict. |

## Objective 9.3a: Identify octet-rule exceptions

### Target understanding

A student can identify which of the three exception categories (incomplete octet, odd-electron molecule, expanded octet) applies to a given molecule, and can recognize a coordinate covalent bond.

### Question guides

**1. Forward — classify an exception**
- Variables & ranges: one example from each of the three categories.
- Constraint: correct category identification with justification.
- Contexts: $\ce{BF3}$, $\ce{NO2}$, $\ce{SF6}$, or similar.
- Formats: multiple-choice.
- Worked instantiation: see the study guide's three category examples.

**2. Inverse — identify a coordinate covalent bond**
- Variables & ranges: a Lewis-acid/base adduct (like $\ce{BF3}$ + $\ce{NH3}$).
- Constraint: student must identify which atom donates both electrons.
- Contexts: the $\ce{BF3}$/$\ce{NH3}$ example or similar.
- Formats: short-answer.
- Worked instantiation: "In the $\ce{BF3}$-$\ce{NH3}$ adduct, which atom donates both electrons to the new B-N bond?" → Nitrogen (from $\ce{NH3}$'s lone pair) — boron has an empty orbital (incomplete octet) that accepts the pair.

**3. Conceptual — why expanded octets are limited to Period 3+**
- Variables & ranges: qualitative.
- Constraint: must reference available $d$ orbitals.
- Contexts: contrasting a Period 2 and Period 3 element.
- Formats: short-answer.
- Worked instantiation: "Why can sulfur (Period 3) form $\ce{SF6}$, but oxygen (Period 2, same group) cannot form an analogous $\ce{OF6}$?" → Period 3 and beyond elements have accessible $d$ orbitals that can participate in bonding, enabling an expanded octet; Period 2 elements like oxygen have no available $d$ orbitals at an accessible energy, so they're strictly limited to an octet (or fewer) of surrounding electrons.

**4. Error analysis**
- Variables & ranges: a student tries to draw an expanded octet for a Period 2 central atom.
- Constraint: correction must state the Period 2 octet-rule restriction.
- Contexts: any Period 2 central atom in a hypothetical expanded structure.
- Formats: short-answer.
- Worked instantiation: "A student attempts to draw a Lewis structure with 10 electrons around a central nitrogen atom. What's wrong?" → Nitrogen is a Period 2 element with no accessible $d$ orbitals, so it cannot exceed an octet (8 electrons) around it — the expanded-octet exception only applies to Period 3 and beyond.

### Design guidance by purpose

| Purpose | Cognitive level | How to design the task |
|---|---|---|
| Homework | Understand | 3–4 octet-exception classification items across all three categories. |
| Group discussion | Analyze | Debate why radicals (odd-electron molecules) tend to be highly reactive. |
| Quiz | Understand | One octet-exception classification item. |
| Exam | Analyze | Combine with Objective 9.2a (draw the actual exception structure, not just classify it). |
| Project/activity | Understand | Students find 2 additional real molecules for each of the three exception categories beyond the study guide's examples. |

## Objective 9.3b: Calculate reaction enthalpy from bond enthalpies

### Target understanding

A student can calculate a gas-phase reaction's enthalpy from tabulated average bond enthalpies, correctly applying the "bonds broken minus bonds formed" formula.

### Question guides

**1. Forward — calculate ΔH from bond enthalpies**
- Variables & ranges: a simple gas-phase reaction with known bond enthalpies for all bonds broken and formed.
- Constraint: correct sign convention (broken minus formed).
- Contexts: any gas-phase reaction with a clear bond-breaking/forming inventory.
- Formats: workout.
- Worked instantiation: "Estimate ΔH for $\ce{H2(g) + Cl2(g) -> 2HCl(g)}$, given bond enthalpies H-H = 436 kJ/mol, Cl-Cl = 242 kJ/mol, H-Cl = 431 kJ/mol." → $\Delta H=(436+242)-(2\times431)=678-862=-184$ kJ/mol.

**2. Inverse — infer an unknown bond enthalpy**
- Variables & ranges: a reaction's overall ΔH and all bond enthalpies except one.
- Constraint: correct algebraic isolation of the unknown.
- Contexts: any reaction with one unknown bond enthalpy.
- Formats: workout.
- Worked instantiation: "If $\ce{H2(g)+I2(g)->2HI(g)}$ has $\Delta H=-9$ kJ/mol, with H-H = 436 kJ/mol and I-I = 151 kJ/mol, find the H-I bond enthalpy." → $-9=(436+151)-2D_{HI} \Rightarrow D_{HI}=(587+9)/2=298$ kJ/mol.

**3. Conceptual — why bond enthalpies used this way give only an estimate**
- Variables & ranges: qualitative.
- Constraint: must reference that tabulated values are *averages* across different molecular environments.
- Contexts: any polyatomic-molecule reaction.
- Formats: short-answer.
- Worked instantiation: see the study guide's O-H bond enthalpy caution (methanol vs. water) — using average bond enthalpies gives only an estimate of $\Delta H$, not the exact value obtainable from standard formation enthalpies.

**4. Error analysis**
- Variables & ranges: a student reverses the sign convention (formed minus broken).
- Constraint: correction must restate the correct formula and explain why.
- Contexts: any bond-enthalpy reaction problem.
- Formats: short-answer.
- Worked instantiation: "A student computes $\Delta H=\sum D_\text{formed}-\sum D_\text{broken}$. What's wrong?" → The correct relationship is bonds broken *minus* bonds formed — breaking bonds always costs energy (positive contribution) and forming bonds always releases it (negative contribution when subtracted); reversing the formula flips the sign of the entire answer.

### Design guidance by purpose

| Purpose | Cognitive level | How to design the task |
|---|---|---|
| Homework | Apply | 3–4 bond-enthalpy reaction-ΔH problems. |
| Group discussion | Understand | Discuss why bond-enthalpy-estimated ΔH values are usually less accurate than standard-formation-enthalpy-based ones (Ch. 6). |
| Quiz | Apply | One bond-enthalpy ΔH calculation. |
| Exam | Analyze | Combine with Chapter 6's Hess's Law/formation-enthalpy method as a cross-check on the same reaction. |
| Project/activity | Apply | Students estimate ΔH for a real combustion reaction using tabulated bond enthalpies and compare to a formation-enthalpy-based value. |

## Rubric Themes for Chapter 9

- **Bond classification requires an explicit ΔEN comparison**, not a memorized "this compound is ionic" fact.
- **Valence-electron counting (including charge adjustment) is the mandatory first step** for every Lewis-structure problem — an otherwise-correct structure built on a miscounted total is not full credit.
- **Formal charge always uses half of bonding electrons per atom**, never the full bonding-electron count.
- **Resonance must be described correctly**: two-plus structures representing one real, stable molecule — never described as the molecule physically switching between forms.
