# Chapter 4 Assessment Guide: Reactions in Aqueous Solutions

## Source and Format

**Reference:** Yu Wang's General Chemistry Lecture Notes, Chapter 4 (CC BY 4.0). **Audience:** introductory undergraduate general chemistry, first semester. **Note:** this is design guidance for building assessments — question *guides* an instructor or generator can instantiate into many concrete questions — not a finished question bank or answer key.

## General Assessment Priorities

- Require every net ionic equation answer to show the intermediate ionic-form step, not just the final net equation — this makes spectator-ion cancellation an explicit, checkable step.
- Test the "weak electrolytes stay undissociated in ionic equations" rule directly and repeatedly — it is the single most common source of net-ionic-equation errors.
- Pair oxidation-number questions with a redox-classification question on the same reaction, so the two skills reinforce each other rather than being tested in isolation.
- Keep molarity/dilution problems dimensionally explicit (units shown at every step), consistent with the dimensional-analysis standard set in Chapter 1.

## Objective 4.1a: Classify solutes as strong, weak, or nonelectrolytes

### Target understanding

A student can classify a given solute as a strong electrolyte, weak electrolyte, or nonelectrolyte based on whether and how completely it dissociates/ionizes in water.

### Question guides

**1. Forward — classify a list of solutes**
- Variables & ranges: a soluble salt, a strong acid, a weak acid, and a molecular nonelectrolyte (e.g., sugar).
- Constraint: include at least one of each category.
- Contexts: common laboratory and household solutes.
- Formats: multiple-choice.
- Worked instantiation: "Classify $\ce{CH3COOH}$ (acetic acid)." → weak electrolyte (weak acid, partial ionization).

**2. Inverse — predict conductivity from classification**
- Variables & ranges: a solute's classification is given; predict whether/how strongly its solution conducts.
- Constraint: must connect ion concentration to conductivity, not just recite the definition.
- Contexts: any classified solute.
- Formats: short-answer.
- Worked instantiation: "Would a 1 M solution of a strong electrolyte or a 1 M solution of a weak electrolyte conduct electricity better? Why?" → The strong electrolyte's solution conducts better — it produces far more ions per mole dissolved, since it dissociates completely.

**3. Conceptual — why "soluble" doesn't mean "strong electrolyte"**
- Variables & ranges: qualitative, contrasting a soluble weak acid with a soluble salt.
- Constraint: must distinguish solubility (how much dissolves) from dissociation (whether it stays as ions once dissolved).
- Contexts: acetic acid vs. sodium chloride.
- Formats: short-answer.
- Worked instantiation: "Acetic acid is quite soluble in water, yet it's a weak electrolyte. How can both be true?" → Solubility describes how much of a substance dissolves; being a weak electrolyte describes what fraction of the dissolved substance ionizes — acetic acid dissolves readily but ionizes only partially.

**4. Error analysis**
- Variables & ranges: a student assumes all ionic compounds are strong electrolytes with no exceptions.
- Constraint: correction must note that this is generally true for *soluble* salts, but insoluble ionic compounds contribute few ions regardless.
- Contexts: a sparingly soluble salt.
- Formats: short-answer.
- Worked instantiation: "A student says all ionic compounds are strong electrolytes. Is there a subtlety here?" → The dissolved portion of any ionic compound does dissociate completely, but if the compound is only sparingly soluble (like $\ce{AgCl}$), very little of it actually dissolves — so its solution conducts weakly not because dissociation is partial, but because there are so few dissolved ions overall.

### Design guidance by purpose

| Purpose | Cognitive level | How to design the task |
|---|---|---|
| Homework | Understand | 4–6 electrolyte-classification items. |
| Group discussion | Understand | Discuss the solubility-vs-dissociation distinction using acetic acid and silver chloride as contrasting cases. |
| Quiz | Understand | One classification set. |
| Exam | Analyze | Combine with Objective 4.2a (electrolyte strength feeds into net-ionic-equation writing). |
| Project/activity | Understand | Students test (or research) the conductivity of several household solutions and classify each. |

## Objective 4.2a: Apply solubility rules to predict whether a compound is soluble

### Target understanding

A student can predict whether an ionic compound is soluble in water using the memorized solubility rules (always-soluble cations/anions, and the specific exceptions).

### Question guides

**1. Forward — predict solubility**
- Variables & ranges: 4–6 ionic compounds spanning the always-soluble rules and the notable exceptions ($\ce{AgCl}$, $\ce{BaSO4}$, etc.).
- Constraint: include at least one exception case.
- Contexts: any ionic compound.
- Formats: multiple-choice.
- Worked instantiation: see the practice sheet (which compound is insoluble: $\ce{Na2CO3}$, $\ce{BaSO4}$, $\ce{AgNO3}$, $\ce{NH4OH}$, $\ce{KOH}$).

**2. Inverse — name a compound satisfying a solubility constraint**
- Variables & ranges: "name a soluble/insoluble compound containing X anion."
- Constraint: must correctly apply the relevant rule, not just guess.
- Contexts: any anion covered by the rules.
- Formats: short-answer.
- Worked instantiation: "Name an insoluble compound containing the sulfate ion." → $\ce{BaSO4}$ (the standard memorized exception).

**3. Conceptual — why exceptions must be memorized rather than derived**
- Variables & ranges: qualitative.
- Constraint: must acknowledge solubility patterns are empirical, not derivable from a single principle at this level.
- Contexts: the $\ce{AgCl}$/$\ce{BaSO4}$ exceptions.
- Formats: short-answer.
- Worked instantiation: "Why can't solubility be predicted from charge alone?" → Solubility depends on the specific balance of lattice energy and hydration energy for each ion pair, which isn't captured by charge alone — this is why specific exceptions must be memorized rather than derived from a simple rule.

**4. Error analysis**
- Variables & ranges: a student assumes all sulfates are soluble with no exception.
- Constraint: correction must name the specific exception.
- Contexts: sulfate compounds.
- Formats: short-answer.
- Worked instantiation: "A student says all sulfate compounds are soluble. What's the exception?" → $\ce{BaSO4}$ (barium sulfate) is the key soluble-sulfate exception to memorize.

### Design guidance by purpose

| Purpose | Cognitive level | How to design the task |
|---|---|---|
| Homework | Remember | 4–6 solubility-prediction items. |
| Group discussion | Understand | Discuss why the exceptions ($\ce{AgCl}$, $\ce{BaSO4}$) are specifically useful in gravimetric analysis. |
| Quiz | Remember | One solubility-prediction item. |
| Exam | Apply | Combine with Objective 4.2b (predict solubility, then write the net ionic equation). |
| Project/activity | Understand | Students build a solubility-rule reference card from the study guide's rule list. |

## Objective 4.2b: Write molecular, ionic, and net ionic equations for precipitation reactions

### Target understanding

A student can write the full molecular equation, expand it into ionic form (dissociating strong electrolytes), and cancel spectator ions to reach the net ionic equation.

### Question guides

**1. Forward — full three-equation sequence**
- Variables & ranges: two soluble reactants forming one insoluble product.
- Constraint: all three equation forms shown, with charge/atom balance checked in the final form.
- Contexts: any precipitation reaction predictable from the solubility rules.
- Formats: workout.
- Worked instantiation: see the study guide's worked examples ($\ce{AgNO3}$/$\ce{NaCl}$; $\ce{Ba(OH)2}$/$\ce{Na2SO4}$).

**2. Inverse — identify spectator ions**
- Variables & ranges: a completed ionic equation.
- Constraint: student must name the spectator ions specifically, not just say "some ions cancel."
- Contexts: any precipitation reaction.
- Formats: short-answer.
- Worked instantiation: "In the reaction forming $\ce{AgCl}$ from $\ce{AgNO3}$ and $\ce{NaCl}$, which ions are spectators?" → $\ce{Na+}$ and $\ce{NO3-}$.

**3. Conceptual — why only the net ionic equation is universal**
- Variables & ranges: qualitative.
- Constraint: must reference that different spectator-ion sources can produce the same net reaction.
- Contexts: any precipitate formable from multiple different soluble salt pairs.
- Formats: short-answer.
- Worked instantiation: "Why is $\ce{Ag+(aq) + Cl-(aq) -> AgCl(s)}$ considered more fundamental than the full molecular equation it came from?" → The same net ionic equation describes AgCl precipitation regardless of which soluble silver salt and which soluble chloride salt were mixed — the spectator ions differ, but the actual chemistry captured by the net ionic equation is identical.

**4. Error analysis**
- Variables & ranges: a student cancels an ion that changed phase (e.g., part of the precipitate) as if it were a spectator.
- Constraint: correction must clarify that only ions unchanged in state on both sides cancel.
- Contexts: any precipitation reaction.
- Formats: short-answer.
- Worked instantiation: "A student cancels $\ce{Ba^2+}$ in the $\ce{BaSO4}$ precipitation reaction, calling it a spectator. What's wrong?" → $\ce{Ba^2+}$ appears as a free (aq) ion on the reactant side but ends up locked inside the solid $\ce{BaSO4}$ precipitate — it did not remain unchanged, so it is not a spectator ion and cannot be canceled.

### Design guidance by purpose

| Purpose | Cognitive level | How to design the task |
|---|---|---|
| Homework | Apply | 3–4 full molecular/ionic/net-ionic sequences. |
| Group discussion | Analyze | Discuss why chemists usually report only the net ionic equation in research contexts. |
| Quiz | Apply | One net-ionic-equation problem. |
| Exam | Analyze | Combine with Objective 4.2a (predict solubility first, then write the equation). |
| Project/activity | Apply | Students predict and write net ionic equations for 3 precipitation reactions from a table of common soluble salts. |

## Objective 4.3a: Distinguish Arrhenius and Brønsted acid/base definitions, and identify common strong and weak acids

### Target understanding

A student can state both definitions of acid and base, identify which species acts as acid/base in a given direction of a reversible reaction, and correctly classify common acids as strong or weak.

### Question guides

**1. Forward — classify a list of acids**
- Variables & ranges: 4–6 acids from the study guide's strong/weak lists.
- Constraint: include $\ce{HSO4-}$ as a specific "watch out" case (weak, despite coming from strong $\ce{H2SO4}$).
- Contexts: the common acids list.
- Formats: multiple-choice.
- Worked instantiation: see the practice sheet (which of $\ce{HNO3}$, $\ce{HI}$, $\ce{HBr}$, $\ce{HCl}$, $\ce{H3PO4}$ is weak).

**2. Inverse — identify acid/base role by direction**
- Variables & ranges: a reversible reaction like $\ce{NH3 + H2O <=> NH4+ + OH-}$.
- Constraint: student must specify forward vs. reverse direction explicitly.
- Contexts: any Brønsted acid-base equilibrium.
- Formats: short-answer.
- Worked instantiation: see the study guide's worked example (water is the acid forward, $\ce{OH-}$ is the base in reverse).

**3. Conceptual — why Brønsted is broader than Arrhenius**
- Variables & ranges: qualitative.
- Constraint: must reference that Brønsted doesn't require water as the solvent.
- Contexts: $\ce{NH3}$ or another Brønsted base that doesn't fit the Arrhenius definition cleanly.
- Formats: short-answer.
- Worked instantiation: "Why is $\ce{NH3}$ considered a base under the Brønsted definition even though it doesn't itself contain $\ce{OH-}$?" → Brønsted defines a base as a proton acceptor; $\ce{NH3}$ accepts a proton from water to form $\ce{NH4+}$ and $\ce{OH-}$ — it doesn't need to already contain hydroxide itself, unlike the stricter Arrhenius definition.

**4. Error analysis**
- Variables & ranges: a student calls $\ce{HSO4-}$ a strong acid because it comes from sulfuric acid.
- Constraint: correction must state that only the first ionization of $\ce{H2SO4}$ is strong.
- Contexts: sulfuric acid's stepwise ionization.
- Formats: short-answer.
- Worked instantiation: "A student calls $\ce{HSO4-}$ a strong acid since it comes from $\ce{H2SO4}$. What's wrong?" → Only $\ce{H2SO4}$'s first ionization (to $\ce{HSO4-}$) is complete/strong; $\ce{HSO4-}$'s own further ionization (to $\ce{SO4^2-}$) is weak, making $\ce{HSO4-}$ itself a weak acid.

### Design guidance by purpose

| Purpose | Cognitive level | How to design the task |
|---|---|---|
| Homework | Remember | 4–6 acid strong/weak classification items. |
| Group discussion | Understand | Discuss the $\ce{HSO4-}$ exception as a specific trap to watch for. |
| Quiz | Remember | One strong/weak acid classification item. |
| Exam | Apply | Combine with Objective 4.3b (identify the acid, then write its neutralization net ionic equation). |
| Project/activity | Understand | Students build a flashcard set of the 7 common strong acids. |

## Objective 4.3b: Write net ionic equations for neutralization reactions

### Target understanding

A student can write a neutralization reaction's net ionic equation, correctly keeping a weak acid or base undissociated while dissociating strong electrolytes.

### Question guides

**1. Forward — strong acid/strong base**
- Variables & ranges: any strong acid + strong base pair.
- Constraint: net ionic equation must reduce to $\ce{H+ + OH- -> H2O}$.
- Contexts: any strong/strong neutralization.
- Formats: workout.
- Worked instantiation: see the study guide's worked example ($\ce{HCl}$/$\ce{NaOH}$).

**2. Inverse — weak acid/strong base**
- Variables & ranges: any weak acid + strong base pair.
- Constraint: the weak acid must stay undissociated in the ionic equation.
- Contexts: any weak/strong neutralization.
- Formats: workout.
- Worked instantiation: see the study guide's worked example ($\ce{HCN}$/$\ce{NaOH}$).

**3. Conceptual — why the two net ionic equations differ**
- Variables & ranges: qualitative, contrasting the strong/strong and weak/strong cases.
- Constraint: must state that only strong electrolytes are written as separated ions.
- Contexts: the two worked examples.
- Formats: short-answer.
- Worked instantiation: "Why doesn't $\ce{HCN + OH- -> CN- + H2O}$ simplify all the way to $\ce{H+ + OH- -> H2O}$, the way the $\ce{HCl}$/$\ce{NaOH}$ reaction does?" → $\ce{HCN}$ is a weak acid and doesn't dissociate completely in solution, so it must be written as the intact molecule $\ce{HCN}$, not as separated $\ce{H+}$ and $\ce{CN-}$ ions.

**4. Error analysis**
- Variables & ranges: a student dissociates a weak acid into ions when writing the ionic equation.
- Constraint: correction must restate the strong-vs-weak electrolyte rule.
- Contexts: any weak-acid neutralization.
- Formats: short-answer.
- Worked instantiation: "A student writes $\ce{HCN}$ as $\ce{H+ + CN-}$ in the ionic equation. What's wrong?" → $\ce{HCN}$ is a weak acid — it does not dissociate completely, so it must remain written as the intact $\ce{HCN}$ molecule, not split into ions.

### Design guidance by purpose

| Purpose | Cognitive level | How to design the task |
|---|---|---|
| Homework | Apply | 3–4 neutralization net-ionic-equation problems, mixing strong/strong and weak/strong pairs. |
| Group discussion | Analyze | Debate what would happen (net equation) for a weak acid + weak base reaction. |
| Quiz | Apply | One neutralization net-ionic-equation problem. |
| Exam | Analyze | Combine with Objective 4.3a (identify strong vs. weak first). |
| Project/activity | Apply | Students write net ionic equations for 3 real over-the-counter antacid neutralization reactions. |

## Objective 4.4a: Determine oxidation numbers

### Target understanding

A student can assign the oxidation number of any atom in a compound or ion using the standard rule set, including the common exceptions (metal hydrides, peroxides).

### Question guides

**1. Forward — determine an oxidation number**
- Variables & ranges: an atom in a neutral compound or polyatomic ion.
- Constraint: student must show which rule(s) were applied.
- Contexts: any compound or ion.
- Formats: workout.
- Worked instantiation: see the practice sheet (S in $\ce{K2SO4}$).

**2. Inverse — identify an exception case**
- Variables & ranges: a metal hydride or peroxide.
- Constraint: student must recognize the exception rather than defaulting to the general rule.
- Contexts: $\ce{LiH}$, $\ce{H2O2}$, or similar.
- Formats: short-answer.
- Worked instantiation: "What is the oxidation number of O in $\ce{H2O2}$?" → $-1$ (the peroxide exception, not the usual $-2$).

**3. Conceptual — why oxidation numbers are a hypothetical bookkeeping device**
- Variables & ranges: qualitative.
- Constraint: must reference the "as if all bonds were 100% ionic" framing.
- Contexts: any covalent compound.
- Formats: short-answer.
- Worked instantiation: "Does an oxidation number represent an atom's actual charge in a covalent molecule?" → Not exactly — it's a hypothetical charge assigned as if every bond were fully ionic, a useful bookkeeping tool rather than a measurement of real charge distribution.

**4. Error analysis**
- Variables & ranges: a student assigns O as $-2$ inside a peroxide.
- Constraint: correction must invoke the peroxide exception.
- Contexts: any peroxide compound.
- Formats: short-answer.
- Worked instantiation: "A student assigns O as $-2$ in $\ce{H2O2}$. What's wrong?" → $\ce{H2O2}$ is a peroxide, where O is an exception at $-1$, not the usual $-2$.

### Design guidance by purpose

| Purpose | Cognitive level | How to design the task |
|---|---|---|
| Homework | Apply | 4–6 oxidation-number problems, including at least one exception case. |
| Group discussion | Understand | Discuss why oxidation number can be a fraction (e.g., superoxide, $-1/2$). |
| Quiz | Apply | One oxidation-number problem. |
| Exam | Analyze | Combine with Objective 4.4b (use the oxidation-number change to justify a redox classification). |
| Project/activity | Apply | Students assign oxidation numbers to every atom in 3 compounds of their choosing. |

## Objective 4.4b: Classify types of redox reactions and use the activity series

### Target understanding

A student can classify a redox reaction as combination, decomposition, combustion, displacement (hydrogen, metal, or halogen), or disproportionation, and can use the activity series to predict whether a displacement reaction will occur.

### Question guides

**1. Forward — classify a redox reaction**
- Variables & ranges: one example from each of the 5 reaction types.
- Constraint: student must name the specific subtype for displacement reactions.
- Contexts: the study guide's own reaction-type examples.
- Formats: multiple-choice.
- Worked instantiation: see the practice sheet ($\ce{2NaI + Br2 -> 2NaBr + I2}$: halogen displacement).

**2. Inverse — predict whether a displacement reaction occurs**
- Variables & ranges: a metal and a metal-ion solution, with their relative activity-series positions given or implied.
- Constraint: student must reference the activity series explicitly to justify yes/no.
- Contexts: any metal displacement scenario.
- Formats: short-answer.
- Worked instantiation: "Will silver metal displace copper from a copper(II) nitrate solution? Why or why not?" → No — silver is *less* active than copper on the activity series, so it cannot displace copper from solution; only a more active metal (like iron or zinc) could do so.

**3. Conceptual — why not every reaction is a redox reaction**
- Variables & ranges: contrast a redox reaction with a non-redox reaction (e.g., a precipitation or simple acid-base reaction).
- Constraint: must reference that oxidation numbers are unchanged in the non-redox example.
- Contexts: any precipitation or neutralization reaction from Objectives 4.2b/4.3b.
- Formats: short-answer.
- Worked instantiation: "Is $\ce{HCl + NaOH -> NaCl + H2O}$ a redox reaction? Why or why not?" → No — every element's oxidation number is unchanged (H stays +1, Cl stays −1, Na stays +1, O stays −2); no electrons are transferred, so this is an acid-base reaction, not a redox reaction.

**4. Error analysis**
- Variables & ranges: a student predicts a displacement reaction will occur in the wrong direction (less active metal "displacing" a more active one).
- Constraint: correction must reference the correct activity-series ordering.
- Contexts: any displacement scenario.
- Formats: short-answer.
- Worked instantiation: "A student predicts copper metal will displace zinc from a zinc sulfate solution. What's wrong?" → Copper is *less* active than zinc on the activity series; only a more active metal (like zinc itself displacing a less active one, or magnesium displacing zinc) can perform a metal displacement — copper cannot displace zinc.

### Design guidance by purpose

| Purpose | Cognitive level | How to design the task |
|---|---|---|
| Homework | Apply | 4–6 redox-classification items across all 5 types. |
| Group discussion | Analyze | Debate which of the 5 redox types is most common in everyday life (combustion is a strong candidate). |
| Quiz | Apply | One redox-classification item. |
| Exam | Analyze | Combine with Objective 4.4a (assign oxidation numbers, then classify the reaction from the changes observed). |
| Project/activity | Evaluate | Students use the activity series to predict the outcome of 3 hypothetical metal/metal-ion combinations, then check their predictions. |

## Objective 4.5a: Calculate molarity and apply the dilution equation

### Target understanding

A student can calculate molarity from moles and volume (or vice versa), and can apply $M_iV_i=M_fV_f$ to determine a dilution volume or resulting concentration.

### Question guides

**1. Forward — mass needed for a target molarity**
- Variables & ranges: a target molarity and volume; mass of solute requested.
- Constraint: full mole-conversion chain shown (mirroring Chapter 3's mole-conversion habit).
- Contexts: any solute with known molar mass.
- Formats: workout.
- Worked instantiation: see the study guide's worked example (glucose solution, 12.7 g).

**2. Inverse — dilution volume**
- Variables & ranges: a stock concentration, target concentration, and target volume.
- Constraint: correct algebraic rearrangement of the dilution equation.
- Contexts: any dilution scenario.
- Formats: workout.
- Worked instantiation: see the study guide's worked example (HCl dilution, 12.8 mL).

**3. Conceptual — why moles of solute don't change on dilution**
- Variables & ranges: qualitative.
- Constraint: must reference that only solvent is added, not solute.
- Contexts: any dilution scenario.
- Formats: short-answer.
- Worked instantiation: "Why does $M_iV_i=M_fV_f$ work for dilution?" → Adding solvent doesn't add or remove any solute — moles of solute stay the same before and after, so $M\times V$ (which equals moles) must be equal on both sides.

**4. Error analysis**
- Variables & ranges: a student rounds an intermediate mole value before multiplying by molar mass.
- Constraint: correction must reference the study guide's own precision caution.
- Contexts: the glucose worked example or similar.
- Formats: short-answer.
- Worked instantiation: see the study guide's own caution about carrying full precision through the glucose-mass calculation (12.7 g, not a source's possibly premature 12.8 g).

### Design guidance by purpose

| Purpose | Cognitive level | How to design the task |
|---|---|---|
| Homework | Apply | 3–4 molarity and 3–4 dilution problems. |
| Group discussion | Understand | Discuss why lab stock solutions are typically kept concentrated and diluted as needed, rather than stored at working concentration. |
| Quiz | Apply | One molarity or dilution problem. |
| Exam | Apply | Combine with Objective 4.5b (use molarity within a full solution-stoichiometry problem). |
| Project/activity | Apply | Students design a dilution protocol to prepare a specific target concentration from a given stock. |

## Objective 4.5b: Apply solution stoichiometry (including ion concentration from a compound's molarity)

### Target understanding

A student can calculate the mass or concentration of a solute given constraints on an ion's concentration (accounting for the compound's ion stoichiometry), and can describe the gravimetric-analysis and titration procedures.

### Question guides

**1. Forward — mass needed for a target ion concentration**
- Variables & ranges: a compound producing more than one ion per formula unit, and a target ion concentration.
- Constraint: student must divide by the correct stoichiometric factor before converting to mass.
- Contexts: any compound with a 1:2 or 1:3 cation:formula-unit ratio.
- Formats: workout.
- Worked instantiation: see the practice sheet ($\ce{Na2SO4}$, target $\ce{[Na+]}$, 3.11 g).

**2. Inverse — describe a gravimetric-analysis procedure**
- Variables & ranges: an unknown ion to be quantified via precipitation.
- Constraint: all 5 steps (dissolve, react, filter, dry, weigh) must be present in the correct order.
- Contexts: any precipitable ion.
- Formats: short-answer.
- Worked instantiation: "Outline the steps of a gravimetric analysis for determining the amount of $\ce{Cl-}$ in a sample." → Dissolve the sample; react with excess $\ce{AgNO3}$ to precipitate $\ce{AgCl}$; filter and dry the precipitate; weigh it; use the $\ce{AgCl}$ formula and mass to calculate moles (and then mass) of $\ce{Cl-}$ originally present.

**3. Conceptual — why the stoichiometric factor matters**
- Variables & ranges: qualitative, contrasting a 1:1 salt with a 1:2 or 2:1 salt.
- Constraint: must explain why ion concentration and compound (formula-unit) concentration differ for non-1:1 salts.
- Contexts: $\ce{Na2SO4}$ or similar.
- Formats: short-answer.
- Worked instantiation: "Why is $\ce{[Na+]}$ twice the molarity of a $\ce{Na2SO4}$ solution, rather than equal to it?" → Each formula unit of $\ce{Na2SO4}$ releases 2 $\ce{Na+}$ ions upon complete dissociation, so the sodium ion concentration is twice the compound's molarity.

**4. Error analysis**
- Variables & ranges: a student forgets the stoichiometric factor and uses the target ion concentration directly as the compound's molarity.
- Constraint: correction must show the missing division/multiplication step.
- Contexts: any non-1:1 ionic compound.
- Formats: short-answer.
- Worked instantiation: "A student computes the mass of $\ce{Na2SO4}$ needed for $\ce{[Na+]}=0.125\,\text{M}$ by treating 0.125 M as the $\ce{Na2SO4}$ molarity directly. What's missing?" → Since each $\ce{Na2SO4}$ gives 2 $\ce{Na+}$, the compound's molarity is half the target ion concentration (0.0625 M), not equal to it — the student's answer would be twice too large.

### Design guidance by purpose

| Purpose | Cognitive level | How to design the task |
|---|---|---|
| Homework | Apply | 2–3 ion-concentration-to-mass problems using non-1:1 compounds. |
| Group discussion | Understand | Discuss why gravimetric analysis requires a precipitate that is both insoluble and easy to filter cleanly. |
| Quiz | Apply | One ion-concentration mass problem. |
| Exam | Analyze | Combine with Objective 4.2a/4.2b (choose a suitable precipitating agent using the solubility rules, then compute the resulting mass). |
| Project/activity | Evaluate | Students design a full gravimetric-analysis protocol for a specific unknown-ion scenario. |

## Rubric Themes for Chapter 4

- **Show the ionic-form intermediate step.** A net ionic equation given without showing the full ionic form first does not receive full credit — the cancellation step must be visible, not skipped.
- **Weak electrolytes never appear as separated ions.** Splitting a weak acid or base into ions in an ionic equation is treated as a conceptual error, not a minor slip.
- **Oxidation-number assignments must cite the specific rule (or exception) used.** A correct number without justification is only partial credit.
- **Solution-stoichiometry answers must account for a compound's ion stoichiometry explicitly**, not assume a 1:1 ion-to-formula-unit ratio by default.
