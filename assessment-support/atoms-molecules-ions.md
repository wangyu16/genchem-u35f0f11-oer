# Chapter 2 Assessment Guide: Atoms, Molecules, and Ions

## Source and Format

**Reference:** Yu Wang's General Chemistry Lecture Notes, Chapter 2 (CC BY 4.0). **Audience:** introductory undergraduate general chemistry, first semester. **Note:** this is design guidance for building assessments — question *guides* an instructor or generator can instantiate into many concrete questions — not a finished question bank or answer key.

## General Assessment Priorities

- Keep atomic-number/mass-number arithmetic (Objective 2.1c) and periodic-table family identification (2.1d) as recurring warm-up items throughout the semester, since both are assumed fluent from Chapter 3 onward.
- Require naming questions (2.3a-c) to include an explicit classification step (ionic vs. molecular vs. acid) before the name is produced — this is the single most common source of naming errors.
- Pair every oxoacid-naming question with its corresponding oxoanion, so students practice the "-ic/-ate" and "-ous/-ite" pairings together rather than in isolation.
- Test isotope notation (Z, A) with the expectation that students will need this exact notation again, unmodified, in Chapter 19.

## Objective 2.1a: State Dalton's atomic theory and describe the subatomic particles

### Target understanding

A student can state the four postulates of Dalton's atomic theory and describe the relative mass and charge of the electron, proton, and neutron.

### Question guides

**1. Forward — state a postulate**
- Variables & ranges: any one of the four postulates, described in a scenario.
- Constraint: answer must identify which specific postulate applies.
- Contexts: any chemical-reaction or compound-composition scenario.
- Formats: short-answer.
- Worked instantiation: "A reaction produces the same total mass of products as reactants. Which Dalton postulate does this reflect?" → Postulate 4 — atoms are only rearranged, never created or destroyed.

**2. Inverse — identify a particle from mass/charge**
- Variables & ranges: given a mass or charge value, name the particle.
- Constraint: recognize protons and neutrons have nearly equal mass, far greater than the electron.
- Contexts: any subatomic-particle table.
- Formats: multiple-choice.
- Worked instantiation: "Which particle has a charge of 0?" → the neutron.

**3. Conceptual — why mass concentrates in the nucleus**
- Variables & ranges: qualitative.
- Constraint: must reference the ~1800× mass ratio.
- Contexts: any atomic-structure discussion.
- Formats: short-answer.
- Worked instantiation: see the study guide's Figure 2.1 (subatomic particle masses).

**4. Error analysis**
- Variables & ranges: a student claims all atoms of an element are identical in mass.
- Constraint: correction must invoke isotopes (Objective 2.1c) as the exception Dalton's original theory didn't anticipate.
- Contexts: any element with well-known isotopes (carbon, uranium).
- Formats: short-answer.
- Worked instantiation: "Dalton said all atoms of one element are identical — is this still considered strictly true?" → Not quite; isotopes of the same element have different numbers of neutrons and therefore different masses, a refinement discovered after Dalton.

### Design guidance by purpose

| Purpose | Cognitive level | How to design the task |
|---|---|---|
| Homework | Understand | 3–4 items covering the four postulates and the particle table. |
| Group discussion | Understand | Discuss which of Dalton's postulates needed later revision (isotopes) and which still hold. |
| Quiz | Remember | One postulate-identification or particle-table item. |
| Exam | Understand | Combine with Objective 2.1c (particle counts feed directly into Z/A). |
| Project/activity | Understand | Students build a timeline of the four historical experiments and what each revealed. |

## Objective 2.1b: Summarize Rutherford's gold-foil experiment and what it revealed about atomic structure

### Target understanding

A student can describe the three observed outcomes of the gold-foil experiment (straight-through, slight deflection, rare bounce-back) and explain what each implies about atomic structure.

### Question guides

**1. Forward — connect an observation to a conclusion**
- Variables & ranges: one of the three observed outcomes.
- Constraint: must state the specific structural implication, not just "atoms have a nucleus."
- Contexts: the gold-foil experiment as described in the study guide.
- Formats: short-answer.
- Worked instantiation: "Most alpha particles passed straight through the foil. What does this imply?" → Most of the atom's volume is empty space.

**2. Inverse — predict an outcome from a proposed model**
- Variables & ranges: a "what if the atom were structured differently" prompt (e.g., plum-pudding model).
- Constraint: must contrast the predicted outcome under the old model with the actual observation.
- Contexts: pre-Rutherford atomic models.
- Formats: short-answer.
- Worked instantiation: "Under the earlier 'plum pudding' model (charge spread evenly through the atom), would you expect any particles to bounce straight back? Why did Rutherford's result rule this out?" → No — spread-out charge could never produce a strong-enough repulsive force at one point; the actual rare bounce-back result requires a concentrated charge (a nucleus).

**3. Conceptual — why only a rare few bounce back**
- Variables & ranges: qualitative, geometric.
- Constraint: must reference the nucleus's small size relative to the whole atom.
- Contexts: the gold-foil experiment.
- Formats: short-answer.
- Worked instantiation: see the study guide's Figure 2.3 and its Reminder badge.

**4. Error analysis**
- Variables & ranges: a student claims Rutherford's experiment discovered the neutron.
- Constraint: correction must attribute the neutron to Chadwick's separate, later experiment.
- Contexts: the four historical experiments as a set.
- Formats: short-answer.
- Worked instantiation: "A student says Rutherford's gold-foil experiment discovered the neutron. What's wrong?" → Rutherford's experiment revealed the nucleus (a small, dense, positively charged core); the neutron itself was discovered later by Chadwick, using a different experiment (beryllium bombarded with alpha particles).

### Design guidance by purpose

| Purpose | Cognitive level | How to design the task |
|---|---|---|
| Homework | Understand | 2–3 items connecting observations to structural conclusions. |
| Group discussion | Analyze | Debate why the "rare bounce-back" result was the most surprising to Rutherford himself. |
| Quiz | Understand | One observation-to-conclusion item. |
| Exam | Analyze | Combine with Objective 2.1a (particle properties) to explain *why* the nucleus repels alpha particles. |
| Project/activity | Understand | Students sketch their own version of the gold-foil diagram before seeing Figure 2.3. |

## Objective 2.1c: Define atomic number, mass number, and isotope, and calculate particle counts

### Target understanding

A student can calculate the number of protons, neutrons, and electrons in a given atom or ion from its atomic number and mass number.

### Question guides

**1. Forward — find neutrons from Z and A**
- Variables & ranges: any element with given Z and A.
- Constraint: correct subtraction.
- Contexts: any isotope.
- Formats: workout.
- Worked instantiation: see the study guide's worked example (U-235).

**2. Inverse — find protons/electrons for an ion**
- Variables & ranges: a monatomic cation or anion.
- Constraint: student must recognize that gaining/losing electrons never changes proton count.
- Contexts: common monatomic ions.
- Formats: workout.
- Worked instantiation: see the study guide's worked example ($\ce{Cl-}$).

**3. Conceptual — isotopes vs. ions**
- Variables & ranges: qualitative contrast.
- Constraint: must distinguish "different neutron count" (isotope) from "different electron count" (ion).
- Contexts: any element with both isotopes and common ions.
- Formats: short-answer.
- Worked instantiation: "How is an isotope different from an ion of the same element?" → An isotope has a different number of *neutrons* (same protons, same electrons if neutral); an ion has a different number of *electrons* (same protons, same neutrons).

**4. Error analysis**
- Variables & ranges: a student subtracts Z from A to find protons instead of neutrons.
- Constraint: correction must restate the correct formulas.
- Contexts: any Z/A calculation.
- Formats: short-answer.
- Worked instantiation: "A student computes A−Z and calls the result 'protons.' What's wrong?" → A−Z gives the number of *neutrons*, not protons; the number of protons is Z itself, by definition.

### Design guidance by purpose

| Purpose | Cognitive level | How to design the task |
|---|---|---|
| Homework | Apply | 4–6 particle-count problems across neutral atoms and ions. |
| Group discussion | Understand | Discuss why isotopes of the same element behave almost identically chemically. |
| Quiz | Apply | One particle-count calculation. |
| Exam | Apply | Combine with Objective 2.2a (ion charge) in the same problem. |
| Project/activity | Understand | Students look up 3 isotopes of one element and tabulate Z, A, and neutron count for each. |

## Objective 2.1d: Identify periods, groups, and named families on the periodic table

### Target understanding

A student can locate periods and groups on the periodic table and correctly name the alkali metals, alkaline earth metals, halogens, and noble gases.

### Question guides

**1. Forward — name the family for a given group**
- Variables & ranges: Group 1A, 2A, 7A, or 8A.
- Constraint: must give the family name, not just the group number.
- Contexts: the periodic table.
- Formats: short-answer.
- Worked instantiation: "What family name is given to Group 7A elements?" → the halogens.

**2. Inverse — identify the group from a family name**
- Variables & ranges: any of the four named families.
- Constraint: must state the group number and at least 2 member elements.
- Contexts: the periodic table.
- Formats: short-answer.
- Worked instantiation: "Name the group number and two elements in the 'noble gases' family." → Group 8A; e.g., He and Ne.

**3. Conceptual — metal, nonmetal, or metalloid**
- Variables & ranges: an element description (conductivity, luster).
- Constraint: must use the property-based definition, not just memorized position.
- Contexts: any main-group element.
- Formats: short-answer.
- Worked instantiation: "An element conducts electricity poorly but better than a typical nonmetal. What category might it be?" → A metalloid — intermediate properties between metals and nonmetals.

**4. Error analysis**
- Variables & ranges: a student confuses "period" and "group."
- Constraint: correction must state which is a row and which is a column.
- Contexts: any periodic-table reference.
- Formats: short-answer.
- Worked instantiation: "A student says sodium and potassium are in the same 'period.' What's wrong?" → They're in the same *group* (1A, a vertical column); a period is a horizontal row.

### Design guidance by purpose

| Purpose | Cognitive level | How to design the task |
|---|---|---|
| Homework | Remember | 4–6 family-identification items. |
| Group discussion | Understand | Discuss why alkali metals and halogens have such distinctly different properties despite being close in atomic number. |
| Quiz | Remember | One family-naming item. |
| Exam | Understand | Combine with Objective 2.3a (metal/nonmetal classification feeds directly into naming method choice). |
| Project/activity | Understand | Students color-code a blank periodic table by the four named families. |

## Objective 2.2a: Distinguish molecules from ions, and cations from anions

### Target understanding

A student can classify a species as a molecule, cation, or anion, and can distinguish monatomic from polyatomic ions.

### Question guides

**1. Forward — classify a list of species**
- Variables & ranges: 4–6 species (a molecule, a monatomic cation, a monatomic anion, a polyatomic ion).
- Constraint: include at least one polyatomic ion.
- Contexts: common compounds.
- Formats: multiple-choice.
- Worked instantiation: "Classify $\ce{NH4+}$." → a polyatomic cation (ammonium).

**2. Inverse — given a classification, name a matching example**
- Variables & ranges: any of the four categories above.
- Constraint: example must not duplicate a study-guide example.
- Contexts: open-ended.
- Formats: short-answer.
- Worked instantiation: "Name a polyatomic anion not mentioned in the study guide." → e.g., sulfate, $\ce{SO4^2-}$.

**3. Conceptual — why cations are smaller than their parent atom**
- Variables & ranges: qualitative.
- Constraint: must reference electron loss reducing electron-electron repulsion.
- Contexts: any cation-forming element.
- Formats: short-answer.
- Worked instantiation: "Why is a sodium cation ($\ce{Na+}$) smaller than a neutral sodium atom?" → Losing an electron reduces electron-electron repulsion, letting the remaining electrons pull in closer to the nucleus.

**4. Error analysis**
- Variables & ranges: a student calls $\ce{Cl-}$ a "molecule" because it has a subscript-free formula.
- Constraint: correction must clarify that a single charged atom is an ion, not a molecule.
- Contexts: monatomic ions.
- Formats: short-answer.
- Worked instantiation: "A student calls $\ce{Cl-}$ a molecule since 'it's just one thing.' What's wrong?" → $\ce{Cl-}$ carries a net charge (it gained an electron), which makes it a monatomic ion, not a molecule; molecules are neutral.

### Design guidance by purpose

| Purpose | Cognitive level | How to design the task |
|---|---|---|
| Homework | Understand | 4–6 classification items. |
| Group discussion | Understand | Discuss why metals tend to form cations and nonmetals tend to form anions. |
| Quiz | Understand | One classification set. |
| Exam | Analyze | Combine with Objective 2.1c (ion particle counts). |
| Project/activity | Understand | Students build flashcards for the 10 most common polyatomic ions. |

## Objective 2.2b: Distinguish molecular from empirical formulas, and determine ionic-compound formulas by charge balance

### Target understanding

A student can reduce a molecular formula to its empirical formula, and can determine an ionic compound's formula so that total charge balances to zero.

### Question guides

**1. Forward — reduce to empirical formula**
- Variables & ranges: a molecular formula with a common factor across all subscripts.
- Constraint: must reduce to the lowest whole-number ratio.
- Contexts: any molecular compound.
- Formats: workout.
- Worked instantiation: see the study guide's worked example ($\ce{H2O2}$ → $\ce{HO}$; glucose → $\ce{CH2O}$).

**2. Inverse — determine an ionic formula from ion charges**
- Variables & ranges: a cation and anion with different-magnitude charges.
- Constraint: student must cross charges as subscripts, then reduce.
- Contexts: any charge-balance problem.
- Formats: workout.
- Worked instantiation: see the study guide's worked example ($\ce{Pb^4+}$/$\ce{O^2-}$ → $\ce{PbO2}$).

**3. Conceptual — why ionic formulas have no "molecule"**
- Variables & ranges: qualitative.
- Constraint: must reference the extended lattice/repeating ratio, not discrete molecules.
- Contexts: any ionic solid.
- Formats: short-answer.
- Worked instantiation: "Does a sample of $\ce{NaCl}$ contain individual 'NaCl molecules'?" → No — it's an extended crystal lattice of alternating ions; the formula $\ce{NaCl}$ states only the simplest ratio (1:1), not a discrete molecular unit.

**4. Error analysis**
- Variables & ranges: a student forgets to reduce a crossed-charge formula to lowest terms.
- Constraint: correction must show the reduction step explicitly.
- Contexts: any charge-balance problem with a common factor.
- Formats: short-answer.
- Worked instantiation: "A student writes $\ce{Pb2O4}$ as the final answer for lead(IV) oxide. What's missing?" → The subscripts share a common factor of 2 and must be reduced to lowest terms: $\ce{PbO2}$.

### Design guidance by purpose

| Purpose | Cognitive level | How to design the task |
|---|---|---|
| Homework | Apply | 3–4 empirical-formula and 3–4 ionic-formula problems. |
| Group discussion | Understand | Discuss why an empirical formula alone can't distinguish glucose from formaldehyde-derived compounds sharing $\ce{CH2O}$. |
| Quiz | Apply | One formula problem of either type. |
| Exam | Apply | Combine with Objective 2.3a (name the compound after determining its formula). |
| Project/activity | Apply | Students determine the formula for 3 ionic compounds given a list of common ion charges. |

## Objective 2.3a: Name ionic compounds, including transition-metal compounds

### Target understanding

A student can name a binary or polyatomic-ion-containing ionic compound, correctly applying a Roman numeral when the metal has variable charge.

### Question guides

**1. Forward — name a compound**
- Variables & ranges: a compound with a fixed-charge metal and one with a variable-charge (transition) metal.
- Constraint: Roman numeral required only for variable-charge metals.
- Contexts: common ionic compounds.
- Formats: workout.
- Worked instantiation: see the study guide's worked example ($\ce{NH4NO3}$, $\ce{Fe3(PO4)2}$).

**2. Inverse — write the formula from a name**
- Variables & ranges: a name including a Roman numeral.
- Constraint: student must use the Roman numeral to determine the correct subscripts.
- Contexts: any transition-metal compound.
- Formats: workout.
- Worked instantiation: "Write the formula for copper(II) chloride." → $\ce{CuCl2}$ (Cu²⁺ balances two Cl⁻).

**3. Conceptual — why some metals need Roman numerals**
- Variables & ranges: qualitative, contrasting Group 1A/2A metals with transition metals.
- Constraint: must reference fixed vs. variable charge.
- Contexts: sodium vs. iron.
- Formats: short-answer.
- Worked instantiation: "Why doesn't sodium chloride need a Roman numeral, but iron chloride does?" → Sodium (Group 1A) always forms Na⁺, a single fixed charge; iron can form Fe²⁺ or Fe³⁺, so the Roman numeral is needed to specify which.

**4. Error analysis**
- Variables & ranges: a student adds a Roman numeral to a fixed-charge metal (e.g., "sodium(I) chloride").
- Constraint: correction must state the Roman numeral is unnecessary/incorrect here.
- Contexts: Group 1A/2A metals.
- Formats: short-answer.
- Worked instantiation: "A student writes 'sodium(I) chloride.' What's wrong?" → Sodium has only one possible charge (+1), so a Roman numeral is unnecessary — the compound is simply named sodium chloride.

### Design guidance by purpose

| Purpose | Cognitive level | How to design the task |
|---|---|---|
| Homework | Apply | 4–6 naming/formula-writing problems, mixed fixed- and variable-charge metals. |
| Group discussion | Understand | Discuss which transition metals are commonly fixed-charge exceptions (e.g., Zn²⁺, Ag⁺). |
| Quiz | Apply | One naming or formula-writing item. |
| Exam | Analyze | Combine with Objective 2.2b (determine the formula, then name it). |
| Project/activity | Apply | Students name 5 ionic compounds found on household product labels. |

## Objective 2.3b: Name binary molecular compounds using Greek prefixes

### Target understanding

A student can name a binary molecular compound using the correct Greek numerical prefixes, applying the "mono-" omission convention for the first element.

### Question guides

**1. Forward — name a compound**
- Variables & ranges: a compound of two nonmetals with a small subscript on each.
- Constraint: correct prefix for each element's count.
- Contexts: common molecular compounds.
- Formats: workout.
- Worked instantiation: "Name $\ce{N2O5}$." → dinitrogen pentoxide.

**2. Inverse — write the formula from a name**
- Variables & ranges: a name using two different prefixes.
- Constraint: correct subscript from each prefix.
- Contexts: any molecular compound.
- Formats: workout.
- Worked instantiation: "Write the formula for sulfur trioxide." → $\ce{SO3}$.

**3. Conceptual — why molecular compounds need prefixes but ionic ones don't**
- Variables & ranges: qualitative, contrasting $\ce{CO}$/$\ce{CO2}$ with a fixed-ratio ionic compound.
- Constraint: must reference that ionic formulas are fully determined by charge balance, while molecular compounds between the same two elements can have several different, equally valid ratios.
- Contexts: carbon oxides vs. sodium chloride.
- Formats: short-answer.
- Worked instantiation: see the study guide's caution against mixing naming systems.

**4. Error analysis**
- Variables & ranges: a student uses a Roman numeral on a molecular compound, or a Greek prefix on an ionic one.
- Constraint: correction must identify the compound type first.
- Contexts: any mixed practice set.
- Formats: short-answer.
- Worked instantiation: "A student names $\ce{SiCl4}$ as 'silicon(IV) chloride.' What's the more standard name?" → Since silicon and chlorine are both nonmetals, this is a molecular compound and should use Greek prefixes: silicon tetrachloride.

### Design guidance by purpose

| Purpose | Cognitive level | How to design the task |
|---|---|---|
| Homework | Apply | 4–6 naming/formula-writing problems. |
| Group discussion | Understand | Discuss why "mono-" is dropped for the first element but kept for the second (e.g., carbon monoxide, not "monocarbon monoxide"). |
| Quiz | Apply | One naming item. |
| Exam | Analyze | Combine with Objective 2.3a (classify ionic vs. molecular first). |
| Project/activity | Apply | Students name all the nitrogen oxides ($\ce{NO}$, $\ce{NO2}$, $\ce{N2O}$, $\ce{N2O3}$, $\ce{N2O4}$, $\ce{N2O5}$). |

## Objective 2.3c: Name acids (including oxoacids/oxoanions), bases, and hydrates

### Target understanding

A student can name an oxoacid relative to one of the five reference "-ic" acids, name its corresponding oxoanion, and name bases and hydrates.

### Question guides

**1. Forward — name an oxoacid relative to a reference**
- Variables & ranges: an oxoacid with one more or fewer O atom than a reference acid.
- Constraint: must correctly identify the reference acid first.
- Contexts: the five reference oxoacids and their relatives.
- Formats: workout.
- Worked instantiation: see the study guide's worked example ($\ce{HBrO}$ → hypobromous acid).

**2. Inverse — name the oxoanion**
- Variables & ranges: the anion derived from a named oxoacid.
- Constraint: "-ic" → "-ate"; "-ous" → "-ite."
- Contexts: any of the reference acid families.
- Formats: workout.
- Worked instantiation: "Name the anion derived from sulfuric acid ($\ce{H2SO4}$)." → sulfate ($\ce{SO4^2-}$).

**3. Conceptual — why "per-" and "hypo-" both modify the same reference direction**
- Variables & ranges: qualitative.
- Constraint: must state "per-" adds oxygen (more than "-ic") while "hypo-...-ous" removes two (fewer than "-ous").
- Contexts: the chlorine oxoacid series (perchloric → chloric → chlorous → hypochlorous).
- Formats: short-answer.
- Worked instantiation: "Rank $\ce{HClO4}$, $\ce{HClO3}$, $\ce{HClO2}$, $\ce{HClO}$ from most to fewest oxygen atoms, and give each name." → perchloric acid ($\ce{HClO4}$) > chloric acid ($\ce{HClO3}$) > chlorous acid ($\ce{HClO2}$) > hypochlorous acid ($\ce{HClO}$).

**4. Error analysis**
- Variables & ranges: a student names a hydrate without the numerical prefix for water molecules.
- Constraint: correction must state the specific prefix needed.
- Contexts: any hydrate.
- Formats: short-answer.
- Worked instantiation: "A student names $\ce{CuSO4\cdot5H2O}$ as just 'copper sulfate hydrate.' What's missing?" → The prefix indicating exactly 5 water molecules: copper(II) sulfate *penta*hydrate.

### Design guidance by purpose

| Purpose | Cognitive level | How to design the task |
|---|---|---|
| Homework | Apply | 4–6 acid/oxoanion naming problems across at least 2 reference families. |
| Group discussion | Analyze | Work through the full chlorine oxoacid/oxoanion series as a group before testing individually. |
| Quiz | Apply | One oxoacid or oxoanion naming item. |
| Exam | Analyze | Combine with Objective 2.2b (write the ion's formula, then name the parent acid or anion). |
| Project/activity | Understand | Students build a reference card with the five "-ic" acids and their per-/-ous/hypo- relatives. |

## Rubric Themes for Chapter 2

- **Classify before naming.** Full credit for any naming question requires (implicitly or explicitly) identifying the compound type — ionic, molecular, or acid — before the naming rule is applied.
- **Roman numerals only where needed.** Adding an unnecessary Roman numeral to a fixed-charge metal, or omitting a required one for a variable-charge metal, is treated as an error either way.
- **Reduce ionic formulas to lowest terms.** A charge-balanced but unreduced formula (e.g., $\ce{Pb2O4}$ instead of $\ce{PbO2}$) is not considered a fully correct final answer.
- **Z/A notation must be exact.** Since this notation reappears unmodified in Chapter 19, sloppy or inconsistent Z/A usage here should be corrected immediately, not deferred.
