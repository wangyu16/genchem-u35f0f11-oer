# Chapter 8 Assessment Guide: Periodic Relationships Among the Elements

## Source and Format

**Reference:** Yu Wang's General Chemistry Lecture Notes, Chapter 8 (CC BY 4.0). **Audience:** introductory undergraduate general chemistry, first semester. **Note:** this is design guidance for building assessments — question *guides* an instructor or generator can instantiate into many concrete questions — not a finished question bank or answer key.

## General Assessment Priorities

- Require every trend-comparison question (radius, IE, EA) to be justified by effective nuclear charge reasoning, not just a memorized directional rule ("bigger is up-left" without explanation is insufficient).
- Test isoelectronic-series reasoning separately from same-element-different-charge reasoning — these two size rules point in different directions and are the most commonly confused pair in the chapter.
- Keep group-survey questions concrete (specific elements, specific reactions) rather than asking students to recite general group properties in the abstract.
- Always require the reasoning behind an oxide's acid/base classification (metallic vs. nonmetallic character), not just a memorized specific-compound answer.

## Objective 8.1a: Classify elements by category

### Target understanding

A student can classify a given element as representative, noble gas, transition metal, Group 2B, lanthanide, or actinide, based on which subshell is being filled.

### Question guides

**1. Forward — classify from element identity**
- Variables & ranges: 4–6 elements spanning all six categories.
- Constraint: include at least one Group 2B element (Zn, Cd, or Hg) as a specific trap.
- Contexts: any element.
- Formats: multiple-choice.
- Worked instantiation: "Classify zinc (Z=30)." → Group 2B — its $3d$ subshell is completely filled, so it's neither a classic transition metal nor a representative element.

**2. Inverse — name an element given a category and constraint**
- Variables & ranges: a category and an additional constraint (e.g., "period 4").
- Constraint: answer must be consistent with both.
- Contexts: any category.
- Formats: short-answer.
- Worked instantiation: "Name a period-4 transition metal." → e.g., iron, or any of Sc–Zn.

**3. Conceptual — why Group 2B is neither representative nor transition**
- Variables & ranges: qualitative.
- Constraint: must reference the completely-filled $d$ subshell.
- Contexts: Zn, Cd, Hg.
- Formats: short-answer.
- Worked instantiation: see the study guide's definition of Group 2B.

**4. Error analysis**
- Variables & ranges: a student calls zinc a transition metal.
- Constraint: correction must state the completely-filled-$d$-subshell distinction.
- Contexts: zinc specifically.
- Formats: short-answer.
- Worked instantiation: "A student calls zinc a transition metal since it's between Groups 2A and 3A visually. What's wrong?" → Transition metals have an *incompletely* filled $d$ subshell (or readily form cations that do); zinc's $3d$ subshell is completely filled ($3d^{10}$) in both the neutral atom and its $\ce{Zn^2+}$ ion, so it's classified separately as a Group 2B element.

### Design guidance by purpose

| Purpose | Cognitive level | How to design the task |
|---|---|---|
| Homework | Remember | 4–6 element-classification items. |
| Group discussion | Understand | Discuss why the lanthanides/actinides are pulled out of the main table body. |
| Quiz | Remember | One classification item. |
| Exam | Understand | Combine with Objective 8.1b (classification determines the ion-formation pattern). |
| Project/activity | Understand | Students color-code a blank periodic table by all six categories. |

## Objective 8.1b: Write electron configurations for ions and identify isoelectronic species

### Target understanding

A student can write the electron configuration of a cation or anion (representative or transition-metal), correctly applying the "4s before 3d" removal order for transition metals, and can identify isoelectronic species.

### Question guides

**1. Forward — representative-element ion configuration**
- Variables & ranges: a cation or anion of a main-group element.
- Constraint: must reach a noble-gas configuration.
- Contexts: any representative element.
- Formats: workout.
- Worked instantiation: see the study guide's examples ($\ce{Na+}$, $\ce{F-}$).

**2. Inverse — transition-metal ion configuration**
- Variables & ranges: a transition metal forming 2+ or 3+ ions.
- Constraint: electrons removed from $ns$ before $(n-1)d$.
- Contexts: any first-row transition metal.
- Formats: workout.
- Worked instantiation: see the study guide's example ($\ce{Fe}$/$\ce{Fe^2+}$/$\ce{Fe^3+}$).

**3. Conceptual — why 4s empties before 3d despite filling first**
- Variables & ranges: qualitative.
- Constraint: must reference that once occupied, $4s$ electrons are actually higher in energy than $3d$ electrons in the ion (the filling-order "crossover" reverses upon ionization).
- Contexts: any transition-metal cation.
- Formats: short-answer.
- Worked instantiation: "Why does iron lose its $4s$ electrons before its $3d$ electrons, even though $4s$ fills before $3d$ in the neutral atom?" → Once electrons occupy the $3d$ and $4s$ orbitals, the effective energy ordering shifts — the $4s$ electrons become higher in energy than the $3d$ electrons in the presence of the filled $3d$ subshell, so they're removed first upon ionization.

**4. Error analysis**
- Variables & ranges: a student removes a $3d$ electron before $4s$ when forming a transition-metal cation.
- Constraint: correction must restate the removal-order rule.
- Contexts: any transition-metal cation.
- Formats: short-answer.
- Worked instantiation: "A student writes $\ce{Fe^2+}$ as $[\ce{Ar}]4s^23d^4$. What's wrong?" → Transition-metal cations always lose $ns$ electrons before $(n-1)d$ electrons; $\ce{Fe^2+}$ should be $[\ce{Ar}]3d^6$ (both $4s$ electrons removed, none from $3d$).

### Design guidance by purpose

| Purpose | Cognitive level | How to design the task |
|---|---|---|
| Homework | Apply | 4–6 ion-configuration problems, mixing representative and transition-metal cases. |
| Group discussion | Understand | Discuss why isoelectronic species can still have very different chemical properties despite identical electron counts. |
| Quiz | Apply | One ion-configuration problem. |
| Exam | Analyze | Combine with Objective 8.2b (ion electron configuration determines isoelectronic-series membership, which determines relative size). |
| Project/activity | Apply | Students find 3 isoelectronic pairs/groups among common ions and verify their electron counts. |

## Objective 8.2a: Explain effective nuclear charge and atomic radius trends

### Target understanding

A student can explain how effective nuclear charge changes across a period and down a group, and can use this to predict relative atomic radii.

### Question guides

**1. Forward — compare atomic radii within a period**
- Variables & ranges: 2–3 elements in the same period.
- Constraint: explanation must reference $Z_\text{eff}$, not just "it's a known trend."
- Contexts: any period.
- Formats: short-answer.
- Worked instantiation: "Which is larger, Na or Cl? Why?" → Na — across Period 3, $Z_\text{eff}$ increases (more protons, similar shielding), pulling electrons in tighter, so atomic radius shrinks from Na to Cl.

**2. Inverse — compare atomic radii within a group**
- Variables & ranges: 2–3 elements in the same group.
- Constraint: explanation must reference added electron shells, not just $Z_\text{eff}$ alone.
- Contexts: any group.
- Formats: short-answer.
- Worked instantiation: "Which is larger, Li or Cs? Why?" → Cs — despite a larger nuclear charge, each added period adds an entire new outer shell, and the increase in electron-nucleus distance dominates over the modest increase in effective nuclear charge felt by the outermost electrons.

**3. Conceptual — comparing radii among elements not in the same row or column**
- Variables & ranges: two elements from the practice-sheet-style diagonal or off-axis comparison.
- Constraint: must reason using the closer periodic-table reference points.
- Contexts: elements like Sb vs. I (same period, different groups).
- Formats: short-answer.
- Worked instantiation: see the practice sheet (comparing Cl, I, Sb, P, Se for largest radius).

**4. Error analysis**
- Variables & ranges: a student claims radius always increases moving right across a period.
- Constraint: correction must state the correct (decreasing) direction.
- Contexts: any period.
- Formats: short-answer.
- Worked instantiation: "A student says atomic radius increases left to right across a period, since 'there are more electrons.'" What's wrong?" → More electrons are added to the *same* shell as $Z_\text{eff}$ also increases; the increasing nuclear pull dominates, so radius actually *decreases* left to right across a period, not increases.

### Design guidance by purpose

| Purpose | Cognitive level | How to design the task |
|---|---|---|
| Homework | Understand | 4–6 atomic-radius comparison items across periods and groups. |
| Group discussion | Analyze | Debate a comparison requiring both period and group reasoning together (e.g., which is bigger, K or Ca?). |
| Quiz | Understand | One radius-comparison item with justification required. |
| Exam | Analyze | Combine with Objective 8.2b (extend the same logic to ions). |
| Project/activity | Understand | Students rank 5 elements scattered across the periodic table by predicted radius, then check against reference data. |

## Objective 8.2b: Compare ionic radii (isoelectronic series and same-element different-charge)

### Target understanding

A student can rank ionic radii within an isoelectronic series (by nuclear charge) and can rank radii of the same element at different oxidation states (by electron count).

### Question guides

**1. Forward — rank an isoelectronic series**
- Variables & ranges: 4–5 isoelectronic ions/atoms.
- Constraint: correct direction (more $Z$ → smaller radius).
- Contexts: any isoelectronic series.
- Formats: workout.
- Worked instantiation: see the study guide's worked example (N³⁻ through Al³⁺).

**2. Inverse — rank same-element different-charge species**
- Variables & ranges: an element and 2–3 of its ions/neutral form.
- Constraint: correct direction (more electrons → larger radius, for fixed $Z$).
- Contexts: a transition metal or main-group element with multiple oxidation states.
- Formats: short-answer.
- Worked instantiation: see the study guide's example ($\ce{Fe^3+}<\ce{Fe^2+}<\ce{Fe}$).

**3. Conceptual — why the two ranking rules point in "opposite" directions**
- Variables & ranges: qualitative, explicitly contrasting the two rules.
- Constraint: must identify what's held constant in each case (electron count vs. nuclear charge).
- Contexts: both worked examples together.
- Formats: short-answer.
- Worked instantiation: "Why does 'more nuclear charge → smaller radius' apply to an isoelectronic series, while 'more electrons → larger radius' applies to same-element ions — aren't these contradictory?" → They're not contradictory — different things are held constant: in an isoelectronic series, electron count is fixed and nuclear charge varies; for same-element ions, nuclear charge is fixed and electron count varies. Each rule is internally consistent once you're clear on which variable is changing.

**4. Error analysis**
- Variables & ranges: a student applies the isoelectronic rule to a same-element-different-charge comparison (or vice versa).
- Constraint: correction must identify which rule actually applies given what's held constant.
- Contexts: a mixed comparison set.
- Formats: short-answer.
- Worked instantiation: "A student ranks $\ce{Fe}$, $\ce{Fe^2+}$, $\ce{Fe^3+}$ using the isoelectronic-series logic (more charge = smaller). Coincidentally this gives the right order — is the reasoning correct?" → Not for the right reason — these three species are *not* isoelectronic (they have different electron counts, all with the same $Z$); the correct rule here is that more electrons (less positive charge) means more electron-electron repulsion and a larger radius, which happens to produce the same ranking but via different reasoning.

### Design guidance by purpose

| Purpose | Cognitive level | How to design the task |
|---|---|---|
| Homework | Apply | 3–4 ionic-radius ranking problems, both types. |
| Group discussion | Analyze | Work through the "why do these rules look contradictory" conceptual item as a group. |
| Quiz | Apply | One ionic-radius ranking problem. |
| Exam | Analyze | Combine both radius-ranking types in a single multi-part question. |
| Project/activity | Apply | Students build a 2-column reference sheet: "isoelectronic series" rules vs. "same-element, different charge" rules. |

## Objective 8.3a: Explain and apply the periodic trend in ionization energy

### Target understanding

A student can describe how ionization energy changes across a period and down a group, explain the exceptions (Be→B, N→O type dips), and apply the trend to rank elements.

### Question guides

**1. Forward — rank elements by first ionization energy**
- Variables & ranges: 4–5 elements, potentially including an exception pair.
- Constraint: correct handling of the Group 2A→3A or Group 5A→6A dip if included.
- Contexts: any set of elements.
- Formats: multiple-choice.
- Worked instantiation: see the practice sheet (highest first IE among C, Ge, O, P, Se).

**2. Inverse — explain a specific dip**
- Variables & ranges: the Be→B or N→O transition.
- Constraint: must reference the specific orbital/subshell reasoning (not just "it's an exception").
- Contexts: Period 2.
- Formats: short-answer.
- Worked instantiation: see the study guide's Figure 8.3 caption (Be→B: 2p vs. paired 2s; N→O: breaking a stable half-filled 2p³).

**3. Conceptual — why successive ionization energies always increase**
- Variables & ranges: qualitative.
- Constraint: must reference the growing positive charge making each subsequent removal harder.
- Contexts: any multi-electron atom.
- Formats: short-answer.
- Worked instantiation: "Why is $IE_2$ always greater than $IE_1$ for the same atom?" → Removing the first electron leaves behind a positively charged ion; removing a second electron from an already-positive species requires overcoming a stronger effective attraction, so it always takes more energy.

**4. Error analysis**
- Variables & ranges: a student ranks Be and B by naive period-trend logic (expecting B > Be) without checking for the exception.
- Constraint: correction must name the specific reason B's IE is lower.
- Contexts: Be vs. B.
- Formats: short-answer.
- Worked instantiation: "A student assumes B has a higher first IE than Be, following the general left-to-right trend. What's the exception here, and why?" → B actually has a *lower* first IE than Be — B's outermost electron is in a $2p$ orbital, higher in energy and less tightly held than Be's paired $2s$ electrons, making it easier to remove despite B having one more proton.

### Design guidance by purpose

| Purpose | Cognitive level | How to design the task |
|---|---|---|
| Homework | Apply | 4–6 ionization-energy ranking and explanation items. |
| Group discussion | Analyze | Work through both Period 2 exceptions (Be→B and N→O) side by side. |
| Quiz | Apply | One IE-ranking item. |
| Exam | Analyze | Combine with Objective 8.2a (same $Z_\text{eff}$ logic, opposite-direction prediction). |
| Project/activity | Evaluate | Students plot IE data for a full period and identify/explain both dips themselves before checking the study guide's explanation. |

## Objective 8.3b: Explain and apply the periodic trend in electron affinity

### Target understanding

A student can describe electron affinity's periodic trend, explain why noble gases are the notable exception, and compare electron affinities across elements.

### Question guides

**1. Forward — compare EA across a period**
- Variables & ranges: 2–3 main-group elements in the same period, excluding noble gases.
- Constraint: correct (generally increasing) direction, left to right.
- Contexts: any period.
- Formats: short-answer.
- Worked instantiation: "Which has a larger electron affinity, Na or Cl?" → Cl — electron affinity generally increases left to right across a period, and halogens in particular have large positive EA since gaining one electron completes their octet.

**2. Inverse — explain why noble gases are the exception**
- Variables & ranges: any noble gas.
- Constraint: must reference the already-filled subshell.
- Contexts: any Group 8A element.
- Formats: short-answer.
- Worked instantiation: "Why do noble gases have near-zero or negative electron affinity, breaking the general periodic trend?" → Noble gases already have a completely filled outer shell; adding another electron would require starting a new, higher-energy shell, which is energetically unfavorable rather than favorable.

**3. Conceptual — why EA is "more complicated" than IE**
- Variables & ranges: qualitative, at an introductory level.
- Constraint: must acknowledge more exceptions exist without needing to enumerate them all.
- Contexts: the general EA trend statement.
- Formats: short-answer.
- Worked instantiation: "The study guide says EA trends are 'more complicated' than IE trends. What's a reasonable takeaway for an intro-level course?" → The overall left-to-right, bottom-to-top increasing trend still holds as a useful first approximation (except noble gases), but more numerous exceptions exist than for IE, so EA predictions should be treated as somewhat less reliable without consulting actual data.

**4. Error analysis**
- Variables & ranges: a student assumes noble gases have the highest electron affinity, by extrapolating the general "increases across a period" rule too far.
- Constraint: correction must flag the noble-gas exception specifically.
- Contexts: any period including its Group 8A member.
- Formats: short-answer.
- Worked instantiation: "A student assumes Ne has the highest electron affinity in Period 2, since it's furthest right. What's wrong?" → Noble gases are a specific, explicit exception to the general trend — Ne's electron affinity is near zero or negative, not the highest in its period; the halogens (Group 7A) typically have the largest positive EA values instead.

### Design guidance by purpose

| Purpose | Cognitive level | How to design the task |
|---|---|---|
| Homework | Understand | 3–4 electron-affinity comparison/explanation items. |
| Group discussion | Understand | Discuss why halogens specifically have such large positive electron affinities. |
| Quiz | Understand | One EA comparison item. |
| Exam | Analyze | Combine with Objective 8.3a (contrast IE and EA trends on the same element set). |
| Project/activity | Understand | Students look up real EA values for a period and identify where the trend "breaks." |

## Objective 8.4a: Describe periodic patterns in chemical behavior and diagonal relationships

### Target understanding

A student can describe the general chemical behavior of each main group and can explain a diagonal relationship using cation charge-density reasoning.

### Question guides

**1. Forward — describe a group's general behavior**
- Variables & ranges: any main group (1A through 8A).
- Constraint: must include at least one specific reaction or compound example, not just an abstract description.
- Contexts: any group.
- Formats: short-answer.
- Worked instantiation: "Describe the general reactivity of Group 7A (the halogens)." → Reactive nonmetals with the formula X₂; never found free in nature; high IE and large positive EA; form halide anions isoelectronic with the adjacent noble gas; react with hydrogen to form hydrogen halides.

**2. Inverse — identify a diagonal relationship pair**
- Variables & ranges: one of Li, Be, B.
- Constraint: student must name the correct diagonal partner (Mg, Al, Si respectively).
- Contexts: the diagonal-relationship examples.
- Formats: short-answer.
- Worked instantiation: "What element shows a diagonal relationship with beryllium?" → Magnesium.

**3. Conceptual — why diagonal relationships exist**
- Variables & ranges: qualitative.
- Constraint: must reference similar cation charge density.
- Contexts: Li/Mg, Be/Al, or B/Si.
- Formats: short-answer.
- Worked instantiation: "Why does beryllium behave somewhat like magnesium, despite being in a different group and period?" → Moving diagonally down-right roughly balances the decrease in charge that comes from moving right with the increase in size that comes from moving down, producing a similar charge-to-size ratio (charge density) on the resulting cations, and therefore similar chemical behavior.

**4. Error analysis**
- Variables & ranges: a student expects boron's chemistry to closely resemble aluminum's, since they're in the same group.
- Constraint: correction must note boron's metalloid nature vs. aluminum's metallic behavior.
- Contexts: Group 3A specifically.
- Formats: short-answer.
- Worked instantiation: "A student expects boron to behave like a typical metal, since it's in the same group as aluminum. What's wrong?" → Boron is a metalloid (the first member of Group 3A), unreactive toward oxygen and water and forming no binary ionic compounds — quite different from aluminum's clearly metallic behavior; Group 3A is exactly the kind of group (per the chapter's own caution) that spans a metal/metalloid transition, so same-group similarity is weaker here.

### Design guidance by purpose

| Purpose | Cognitive level | How to design the task |
|---|---|---|
| Homework | Understand | 4–6 group-behavior description items across different groups. |
| Group discussion | Analyze | Debate why Groups 1A/2A and 7A/8A are the "most reliable" for same-group similarity, per the chapter's own caution. |
| Quiz | Understand | One group-behavior or diagonal-relationship item. |
| Exam | Analyze | Combine with Objective 8.4b (group behavior connects directly to oxide acidity/basicity). |
| Project/activity | Understand | Students research a diagonal-relationship pair not explicitly covered (e.g., B/Si) and find a specific shared property. |

## Objective 8.4b: Classify metal, nonmetal, and amphoteric oxides

### Target understanding

A student can classify an oxide as acidic, basic, or amphoteric based on the parent element's metallic character, and can identify $\ce{Al2O3}$ as the standard amphoteric example.

### Question guides

**1. Forward — classify a list of oxides**
- Variables & ranges: 4–6 oxides spanning metal, nonmetal, and amphoteric.
- Constraint: include $\ce{Al2O3}$ specifically.
- Contexts: any set of common oxides.
- Formats: multiple-choice.
- Worked instantiation: see the practice sheet (identifying the basic oxide among 5 options).

**2. Inverse — predict a reaction from a classification**
- Variables & ranges: a classified oxide (acidic, basic, or amphoteric) and a reagent (acid or base).
- Constraint: student must correctly predict whether a reaction occurs.
- Contexts: any classified oxide.
- Formats: short-answer.
- Worked instantiation: "Would $\ce{MgO}$ react with hydrochloric acid? With sodium hydroxide?" → It would react with HCl (a basic oxide reacts with an acid to form a salt and water) but not meaningfully with NaOH (a base doesn't react with another base).

**3. Conceptual — why $\ce{Al2O3}$ is amphoteric**
- Variables & ranges: qualitative.
- Constraint: must reference aluminum's borderline metallic character (or its position at the metal/metalloid boundary region).
- Contexts: $\ce{Al2O3}$ specifically.
- Formats: short-answer.
- Worked instantiation: "Why is $\ce{Al2O3}$ amphoteric rather than clearly basic or acidic?" → Aluminum sits near the boundary between metallic and nonmetallic character on the periodic table, giving its oxide intermediate properties — able to react as either an acid (with a strong base) or a base (with a strong acid), unlike the clearly one-sided oxides of elements further from that boundary.

**4. Error analysis**
- Variables & ranges: a student classifies a metalloid oxide (like $\ce{SiO2}$) as basic, by analogy with metal oxides.
- Constraint: correction must state that $\ce{SiO2}$ is actually acidic.
- Contexts: $\ce{SiO2}$ specifically.
- Formats: short-answer.
- Worked instantiation: "A student assumes $\ce{SiO2}$ (silicon dioxide) is basic since silicon is 'almost a metal.' What's wrong?" → $\ce{SiO2}$ is actually classified as acidic — silicon's metalloid/nonmetal-leaning character across the period means its oxide reacts with bases, not acids, despite silicon's intermediate metallic character.

### Design guidance by purpose

| Purpose | Cognitive level | How to design the task |
|---|---|---|
| Homework | Apply | 4–6 oxide-classification items. |
| Group discussion | Understand | Discuss why oxide acidity/basicity is a useful quick proxy for metallic character across a period. |
| Quiz | Apply | One oxide-classification item. |
| Exam | Analyze | Combine with Objective 8.4a (connect oxide behavior back to the parent element's group-level chemical behavior). |
| Project/activity | Evaluate | Students classify the oxides of every Period 3 element and identify the metal-to-nonmetal transition point. |

## Rubric Themes for Chapter 8

- **Every trend claim requires effective-nuclear-charge reasoning**, not a memorized directional rule stated without explanation.
- **Isoelectronic-series and same-element-different-charge radius rules must be applied to the correct scenario** — conflating them (even when the numeric ranking happens to come out right) is treated as a reasoning error.
- **Exceptions (Be→B, N→O in IE; noble gases in EA) must be named specifically when relevant**, not glossed over as "it just doesn't follow the trend here."
- **Oxide classification requires the metallic-character justification**, not just a memorized specific-compound answer.
